What the 16bp adjustment below is, precisely. It is added to the MODEL'S OWN ESTIMATE of where the market clears — not to our quotes, and not to any historical record, which are both left exactly as recorded. The reason an adjustment is needed at all: when we lose a deal on price, our records show only that the market cleared somewhere below our quote, never the actual level. Because our quotes are not set at random — we price tighter when we sense a competitive deal — that one-sided view pulls the model's estimate of the market a little too low. We confirmed the size and the direction by rebuilding the test data with a quote that does not depend on our own read of the market, which removes most of the same gap. The 16bp correction restores what that blind spot would otherwise take away, so the floors, quotes and windows below are not computed against an artificially tight view of the market. It is a measured figure, not a judgement call — but it was measured on synthetic data and must be re-measured on real deal history before it is used to price an actual transaction. Full derivation: Methodology & Data, “Why a correction is needed at all”. Internal: contains our floor, competitor estimates and market intelligence throughout.

About this console — inputs, outputs, fields and method

What this view is for

Four questions a desk head has that no single deal can answer: which segments we can actually win in, who the team should be calling, what rate to put on paper today for something that signs in six months, and whether a losing segment is losing on price or on execution. Each needs a view across the whole book, which is why none of them appear on a client page.

Inputs it reads

  • Every client on the book with rating, country rating, segment and sector.
  • Their traded bonds, from which an issuer credit curve is stripped per name and our actual loan schedule repriced off it.
  • Five years of pricing-committee records, deal announcements and market intel, fitted jointly into one model of where deals clear.
  • The profitability engine's minimum-price matrix (RoRWA ≥ 2%), month by month, so the floor used is the floor that applied at the time.
  • The forward swap curve, and a two-factor model of credit conditions fitted to the spread and volatility panel.

What it produces

  • A map of which rating × tenor × currency segments beat the borrower's own cost of issuing, and which cannot.
  • A ranked call list: headroom against win probability, sized by value.
  • A quote band for a reference credit that still works at signing, in a central case and under a joint stress.
  • Win and loss rates by segment, split by whether the loss was about price.

Field by field

advantage (panel 1)What a new bond would cost this borrower, minus what we would quote. Positive means we are the cheaper option. Shown as a median across sampled clients in each rating × tenor cell.
headroom (panel 2)Where a standard deal is expected to clear, minus our floor. Positive means a deal is possible; negative means our hurdle exceeds the borrower's alternatives.
P(win) (panel 2)Probability of winning at the expected-value-maximising quote, from the clearing distribution plus this client's estimated non-price advantage.
bubble size (panel 2)Expected value of the deal in euros — probability times margin above the hurdle times size times life.
window colour (panel 2)Whether room exists today and whether it still exists in six months. The state is named in every tooltip, so nothing is carried by colour alone.
floor p50 / p90 (panel 3)Our minimum price at its central and its adverse case. p90 is the hurdle we must still clear if funding costs move against us.
clearing p50 / p10 (panel 3)Where the market is forecast to clear, centrally and at its tight end. p10 is the level we must still beat if spreads tighten.
quote band (panel 3)Spreads that satisfy both bounds at that horizon. Where it closes, no single fixed quote survives and the honest answer is a shorter validity or a flex clause.
lost on price vs other (panel 4)Committee-recorded loss reason. These are different failures with different owners — one is a pricing question, the other is structuring, speed or relationship.

The method, and why it is this method

Where the clearing level comes from. A hierarchical Bayesian model of the best competing offer, estimated from our own history. Win/loss outcomes are treated as censored rather than binary: a win says the competing offer was above our price, a loss on price says it was below, and we rarely learn the number. Losses are separated by reason, because a deal lost to a bond issue carries no information about price and pooling it flattens the price response. Sparse cells — and with about 20 closed deals a year across 200 clients and 30 markets most cells are sparse — borrow strength from their sector×rating and country cohort.

Where the bond comparison comes from. Rather than reading a spread curve at the loan's average life, we fit a default-intensity curve through the borrower's whole bond set and reprice our actual repayment schedule off it. This matters: a seven-year-average-life amortising loan matures around thirteen years, so it carries more exposure to the far end of the curve than a seven-year bullet. On our tests the shortcut understates the spread by 9–16% and, worse, understates it systematically — it would make us price tight without ever looking wrong.

Why the bond market is treated as a competitor. A borrower with market access will not pay a bank more than it costs them to issue, so their all-in issuance cost caps where any loan can clear. Without that constraint the optimiser cheerfully recommends prices no treasurer would accept.

Why the forward curve and not a rate forecast. Forward rates are the market's own expectation, they cost nothing, and a proprietary rate view here would be model risk we do not need. Only our own cost of funds and the credit factors are forecast, because only those are genuinely specific to us. The forward curve panel usually looks almost flat over six months, and that is the market telling us it does not expect much to change at this horizon — a flat line here is the forward curve doing its job, not a rendering issue.

Why panel 1 is a percentage and panel 3 is basis points. A base rate and a credit spread are different things measured on different scales. Showing them on one shared axis would make the point where their lines happen to cross look like a meaningful decision boundary, when it would only be an accident of scale — the same reason this leg never uses a dual-axis chart. Two panels, two units, one shared timeline underneath, is the fix.

Why three bands, not one. The central band (floor p50 to clearing p50) is the expected case; the likely band (p75/p25) adds a realistic single-sided move; the joint-stress band (p90/p10) asks whether a quote survives funding costs and the market moving against us at the same time. Reading only the widest band understates how much room there usually is; reading only the narrowest hides the tail risk. When even the joint-stress band closes, that is the signal to shorten the quote's validity or add a flex clause, not to abandon the deal — the central band is very likely still open.

3 · What rate to set today

The question this answers

What rate do we put on paper today for a deal that signs in six months? Quote inside the band and it still works then. Where the stressed band closes, no fixed quote survives and the answer is a shorter validity or a flex clause, not a sharper number.

∑

Base rate — read directly off today's forward swap curve, not forecast: forward rates are the market's own expectation and cost nothing to use. Floor path — our cost of funds, mean-reverting toward its trailing 12-month average, simulated 900 times. Clearing path — a two-factor credit model fitted to the iTraxx / CDX / MOVE / VIX panel, with estimated monthly persistence of 0.82 and 0.72 (half-life ≈3.5 and ≈2.1 months), also simulated 900 times. The three shaded bands are the 50th, 25th/75th and 10th/90th percentiles of those 900 draws at each horizon, not three separate models. Full specification: Methodology & Data, “M5 — the forward view”.

Reference credit: BBB, 6y WAL, EUR, senior unsecured

A quote made today is judged months later, so the question is not "what is the price now" but "what price still works then". The green band is bounded below by our floor at its p90 (the hurdle we must still clear if funding costs move against us) and above by where the market is forecast to clear at its p10 (the level we must still beat if spreads tighten). Quote inside it and the deal survives both. Where the band closes, no quote survives and the honest move is a shorter validity or a flex clause. Base rates come from the forward curve — we do not forecast them, and presenting a proprietary rate view here would be unnecessary model risk.

4 · Price policy vs execution

The question this answers

Where is our price policy wrong, and where is it fine? A segment that loses mostly on non-price grounds will not be rescued by a lower floor — and cutting price to fix an execution failure is how a desk gives away margin for nothing.

Outcomes by region, as a share of offers made

Outcomes by region, as a share of offers made. Lost on price and lost for other reasons are different problems with different owners: the first is a pricing question, the second is structuring, speed or relationship, and averaging them is how a desk ends up cutting price to fix an execution problem. A segment that loses mostly on non-price grounds will not be rescued by a tighter floor — and a region that loses mostly on price, rather than on execution, is where a lower floor would genuinely help.
