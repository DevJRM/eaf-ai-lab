Live pipeline

Every offer on the table, ordered by what it is worth probability-weighted. The last column is a read on which way the risk runs, not an instruction to reprice.

About the live pipeline — inputs, outputs, fields and method

What this view is for

One row per offer currently on the table. It answers two questions: which way does the risk run on this price, and what is this offer worth. It deliberately does not tell you what to charge — that is the committee's call, and a view that issues instructions gets ignored the first time it is wrong about a name the origination team knows better.

Inputs it reads

  • Pricing-committee records: the spread we proposed, size, WAL, currency, amortisation, and the floor that applied at the time.
  • The borrower's traded bonds, from which M0 strips an issuer credit curve and reprices our actual loan schedule off it.
  • The fitted clearing model: five years of our own win/loss outcomes, deal announcements, and market intel, estimated jointly.
  • The profitability engine's minimum-price matrix (RoRWA ≥ 2%), point-in-time.

What it produces

  • Where our quote sits relative to the distribution of where comparable deals clear, in basis points and in words.
  • Expected value of the offer as priced, in euros.
  • The value uplift available if the offer moved to its expected-value-maximising price — the cost of the current price, quantified.

Field by field

our quoteThe spread we have on the table, in basis points over the floating benchmark.
marketMedian of the modelled clearing distribution: where comparable deals for this borrower, tenor and currency are expected to price.
market range10th to 90th percentile of that distribution. Width is genuine uncertainty, not decoration — a 60bp range means we do not know the market to better than 60bp.
floorMinimum spread reaching 2% RoRWA net of cost of funds, for this scenario, as of the committee date. A hard constraint, not a target.
P(win)Probability of winning at our quoted price, from the clearing distribution plus this client's estimated non-price advantage.
EV EURmP(win) × (our quote − floor) × size × WAL. Probability-weighted margin above the hurdle, over the life of the loan, in euros.
how we compareA diagnosis of which way the risk runs: expensive and likely to lose on price, at market, or winning cheaply and leaving spread behind.

The method, and why it is this method

The clearing distribution comes from a hierarchical Bayesian model of the best competing offer. Three things make it different from a win-rate regression:

  • Outcomes are censored, not binary. A win tells us the competing offer was above our price; a loss on price tells us it was below. We almost never see the number. Both are treated as interval observations of one latent quantity rather than as a yes/no label.
  • Losses are separated by reason. A deal lost because a competitor undercut us is informative about price; one lost because the borrower issued a bond or stayed with an incumbent is not. Pooling them flattens the price response in proportion to how many non-price losses are in the sample — about a third of all losses here.
  • Sparse cells borrow strength. With roughly 20 closed deals a year across 200 clients and 30 markets, most client-tenor-currency cells hold one observation or none. Estimates are pooled across client, sector×rating and country, so a thinly-covered name inherits its cohort rather than its own noise. The evidence reading on the client dossier says how much of any given number is data and how much is that pooling.

Expected value is deliberately plain: probability times money. An earlier version divided it by estimated banker-hours, which added a denominator nobody could verify and hid the quantity people actually budget in.

Which of our live offers are we about to lose on price, and which are we winning more cheaply than we needed to? Both are expensive mistakes and they point in opposite directions, so a single “good/bad” score would hide one of them.

About the window monitor — what a "window" is, and what to do about it

What this view is for

This is not a pricing view. It answers a prior question: is a deal with this borrower possible at all right now?

A window is open when some spread exists that does two things at once — clears our 2% RoRWA floor, and still beats what the borrower can get elsewhere (a competing bank, or issuing a bond). When our floor sits above the borrower's alternatives, no such spread exists: the window is closed, and no amount of negotiation opens it. That is a different problem from being expensive, and it has a different answer.

It runs over every client, not only those with a live offer, on one standard configuration each so the names are comparable. That is what makes it a call list rather than a status report on deals already in motion.

Inputs it reads

  • Every client on the book, with its rating, country rating and segment.
  • A standard configuration per segment (EUR, senior unsecured, bullet, 250m, segment-typical WAL) so that differences between rows are differences between borrowers rather than between deal structures.
  • The profitability engine's floor today, and a six-month forecast of our cost of funds, which is what moves it.
  • A two-factor mean-reverting model of credit conditions, fitted to the spread and volatility panel, which is what moves the clearing level.

What it produces

  • P(room exists) today and in six months, per client.
  • The trajectory: whether that room is opening, holding, narrowing or absent.
  • A ranked call list, narrowing names first.

Field by field

marketWhere a standard deal for this borrower is expected to clear.
our floorMinimum spread reaching 2% RoRWA for that same configuration, today.
roomMarket minus floor. Positive means a deal is possible; negative means our hurdle exceeds what the borrower can get elsewhere.
P(room) nowProbability that room is positive today, given uncertainty in both the clearing level and the floor. Evaluated jointly, because the two move with overlapping drivers — treating them as independent would overstate how often a window is open.
P(room) 6mThe same probability at the horizon a deal would actually sign, with the floor drifting on forecast cost of funds and the clearing level on forecast credit conditions.
statusThe four states below, each with the action it implies.
curve basisWhether the borrower's own traded bonds supported the estimate, or it leaned on a sector×rating peer curve. A peer curve is weaker evidence and must never be shown to a client as "your curve".

The method, and why it is this method

Rates are taken from the forward curve rather than forecast. The forwards are the market's own expectation, they are free, and presenting a proprietary rate view here would be unnecessary model risk. Only two things are forecast, because only two are genuinely idiosyncratic: our own cost of funds, which moves the floor, and the credit factors, which move the clearing level.

Credit spreads are close to a random walk at a six-month horizon, so a point forecast here would add little and we do not claim one. The value is in the probability and in the co-movement: floor and clearing level are driven by overlapping factors, and the thing worth knowing is how often they cross, not where either one lands.

Known limitation, stated plainly: on the current synthetic data the credit factor is mean-reverting, so P(room) barely moves over six months and almost nothing registers as narrowing. That is a property of the test data, not a finding about the book. Read the narrowing count as untested until this runs on real history.
