About this dossier — inputs, outputs, fields and method

What this view is for

One page for one borrower, built to answer four questions in the order a originator actually hits them: where to pitch, what to quote, how to defend the number, and when to call. Everything here is derived — there are no facts on this page, only estimates with stated evidence behind them.

Inputs it reads

  • This borrower's traded bonds: maturity, coupon type, currency, seniority, guarantee, size, bid/ask and days since last trade.
  • The EUR and USD swap curves, and the cross-currency basis.
  • The profitability engine's minimum-price matrix (RoRWA ≥ 2%) for this rating, country rating, tenor and currency.
  • The fitted clearing model, estimated across the whole book from five years of committee records, deal announcements and market intel.
  • A new-issue concession table and issuance-cost assumption, for what a bond would cost this borrower.

What it produces

  • A pitchable-scenario map across currency, tenor and amortisation.
  • A quote ladder: walk-away, recommended, stretch, with win probability at each.
  • The borrower's own credit curve with our loan-equivalent spread placed on it.
  • A six-month window read, with quote shelf-life in days.
  • An audit of which of their bonds were used and why the rest were rejected.

Field by field

fair valueWhat this borrower's own bonds imply for a loan of this exact shape. Not a spread read off a curve — a default-intensity curve is fitted through their whole bond set and our actual repayment schedule is repriced off it.
bond cost / new bond all-inWhat issuing would cost them: their secondary curve at this average life, plus a new-issue concession, plus amortised issuance costs, less the carry benefit of drawing a loan against milestones rather than taking a bond's proceeds on day one.
advantageBond cost minus what we would quote. Positive means we are the cheaper option and the pitch has a number behind it. Negative means they should issue.
structurally blockedOur floor is above their bond cost in that cell. Not an expensive deal — an impossible one, which is worth knowing in October rather than in April.
floor / walk-awayMinimum spread reaching 2% RoRWA net of cost of funds. A hard constraint: the shaded region on the frontier is not quotable at any win probability.
recommendedThe spread where P(win) × margin above the hurdle peaks — not where either one peaks alone.
stretchThe widest spread still retaining a credible win probability. Where to open.
P(win)From the modelled distribution of the best competing offer, plus this client's estimated non-price advantage — relationship, speed, structuring, ECA access.
P(window open)Probability that some spread exists which both clears our hurdle and beats the borrower's alternatives. Evaluated jointly, because our floor and the clearing level move with overlapping drivers.
shelf-lifeExpected days before this quote stops clearing the hurdle or stops being competitive. Right-censored at the forecast horizon.
evidenceHow much of the number is this borrower's own data and how much is their cohort's. With roughly 20 closed deals a year across the book, thin names borrow heavily from their sector×rating and country peers, and the badge says so rather than hiding it.

The method, and why it is this method

Why the schedule is repriced instead of read off a curve. A seven-year-average-life amortising loan matures around thirteen years, so it carries more exposure to the far end of the credit curve than a seven-year bullet. Reading a spread curve at the average life misses that by 9–16% of the spread on our tests — and misses it systematically downward, which would make us price tight while never looking wrong.

Why the bond market is a competitor, not just a benchmark. A borrower with market access will not pay us more than it costs them to issue, so their all-in issuance cost caps where any loan can clear. That cap is applied before any recommendation is made.

How win probability is estimated. From our own win/loss record, treated as censored rather than binary: a win tells us the competing offer was above our price, a loss on price tells us it was below, and we rarely learn the number. Deals lost for non-price reasons are excluded from the price response, because including them flattens it — about a third of all losses here are non-price.

What is corrected, and why. Because our own quote decides whether we win, the record is always cut off at our own price. That one-sidedness makes a raw estimate of where the market clears come out too low, so these views add a measured correction back. It is measured on synthetic data and must be re-measured on real history.
