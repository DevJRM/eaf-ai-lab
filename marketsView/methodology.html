How the model works

Organised the same way the model itself is layered: curve and bond mechanics first, then the clearing model, then the floor, then the pricing decision, then the forward view. None of this is borrower-specific — it applies identically to every client's dossier.

M0Fair value from the borrower's own bonds
Reference — the short version

For a given borrower, currency, average life (WAL) and amortisation profile, M0 answers one question: what does this borrower's own bond market imply for a loan of this exact shape?

Why a bond spread cannot be read directly off a curve

The standard shortcut — fit a line through an issuer's bond spreads against maturity and read it at the loan's average life — is wrong for a specific, mechanical reason. A loan with a 7-year average life that amortises typically matures around 13 years, not 7: its principal is repaid progressively, so its exposure to the borrower's credit risk is weighted differently across time than a 7-year bullet bond's is. Reading a spread curve at the average life treats the two as identical. They are not, and the gap between them grows with the steepness of the borrower's own credit curve.

What we do instead

  1. Bootstrap the EUR and USD swap curves from quoted rates — the standard funding-cost reference, not government bonds, for reasons the Tutorial tab covers — so every bond and every loan is discounted consistently.
  2. Fit a piecewise default-intensity (hazard) curve through the borrower's whole bond set — not a spread curve, a survival-probability curve — under a stated recovery assumption (40%, the long-run senior-unsecured average).
  3. Reprice the loan's actual repayment schedule against that hazard curve: every cashflow, on its own date, discounted and survival-weighted individually.
  4. Solve for the flat spread that makes the repriced loan worth par. That number is the loan-equivalent fair value.

What this is measured against

On held-out test data, this method scores 7.3bp RMSE against the known truth, versus 26.0bp for the shortcut — a 72% reduction. More importantly, the shortcut is biased -10.6bp, meaning it is not merely noisier, it is wrong in a consistent direction: too tight, which is the direction that destroys capital rather than merely losing a deal. M0 itself is unbiased (-0.1bp).

Currency and seniority are handled before any fitting happens

A hazard curve belongs to the issuer, not to one bond's currency: the same borrower's EUR and USD bonds imply the same default intensity, and each is stripped against its own currency's discount curve before the two are pooled. Subordinated or guaranteed paper is never used as a stand-in for senior unsecured risk — an earlier version of this leg allowed a parent-guaranteed bond to benchmark an unguaranteed loan, which understates the issuer's own standalone risk and biases the result tight; the admission rules are asymmetric specifically to prevent that.

Open, logged, not yet fixed: recovery is one number for two different things

The 40% recovery assumption above is used both to strip the comparable bonds and to reprice the loan. That mostly cancels when the two share a seniority regime — but our loans are typically senior secured or asset-backed while the comparable bonds are typically senior unsecured, and published recovery-by-seniority studies put secured recovery meaningfully higher. One flat number for both likely understates the loan's own seniority benefit. Logged as a pending fix (seniority-split recovery, plus a dedicated mismatch-sensitivity test) in PRICING_BENCHMARK_ARCHITECTURE.md §11 — not yet built.

Tutorial — built up from first principles

Building this up from first principles, in order:

1. Discounting, from scratch

A euro promised in five years is worth less than a euro today, purely from the passage of time — a discount factor DF(t) < 1 converts a future cashflow into today's money: PV = cashflow × DF(t). Every valuation here is a sum of cashflows, each multiplied by its own discount factor.

2. The reference curve: why the swap curve, specifically

A swap exchanges a fixed rate for a floating reference rate (Euribor) over several years. The fixed rate that makes that exchange fair today — the par swap rate — is, maturity by maturity, the market's cleanest read of pure time value of money: it carries no single company's credit risk, and unlike a government bond it is not distorted by safe-haven demand or repo scarcity. bootstrap_curve() solves for this curve sequentially: find the shortest maturity's rate first, then the next, each one chosen so that maturity's swap reprices to exactly zero value today, building the curve maturity-by-maturity the same way a chain of dependent equations gets solved one link at a time.

3. Why a raw YTM, or even a raw z-spread, is not enough

A bond's yield-to-maturity bundles time value and credit risk into one number. A z-spread — the flat add-on to the swap curve that reprices the bond — isolates the credit piece, but is still contaminated by that specific bond's own coupon and schedule: two bonds from the same issuer, same maturity, different coupons, can show different z-spreads for identical credit risk. Something cleaner than either is needed.

4. Survival probability, built from a simple story

Picture a small, constant chance of default each year. Surviving five years means surviving year one, and year two, and… — probabilities multiply. Shrink the time step toward zero and that discrete story becomes a clean formula: S(t) = exp(−∫λ), where λ(t) is the hazard rate — the instantaneous chance of default at each moment — exactly the same limiting argument that turns compound interest into continuous compounding. This system fits λ as piecewise-constant over three maturity buckets per issuer.

5. The full valuation equation

A risky cashflow's value is its survival-weighted, discounted payment, plus a recovery leg paid if default happens first: recovery rate × the balance outstanding at that moment — not the original face value. That distinction only matters for amortisers, where the outstanding balance has already shrunk by the time a default could occur, and it is exactly why an amortising loan cannot borrow a bullet bond's number unmodified.

6. Calibration is just least squares

fit_hazard() searches for the one hazard triple that reprices every admitted bond from an issuer simultaneously as closely as possible — minimising total squared repricing error, the same basic idea as fitting a line through scattered points, except the relationship between λ and price is non-linear, so it is solved numerically rather than with a closed-form formula.

7. Shrinkage: lean on peers when data is thin

A two-bond issuer cannot reliably pin down how its curve slopes. Its slope is blended toward its sector×rating peers' slope, weight n/(n+k) — more of the issuer's own curve as it has more bonds, more of the peer curve when it has few. The curve's level is left unshrunk even with one or two bonds, because the overall level is pinned down much faster than its shape.

8. Why the loan is repriced, not adjusted

Once calibrated, (λ, the discount curve) is a general-purpose pricing formula that takes any cashflow schedule and returns its fair value — it has no notion of "this came from a bond." The correct gap between a bond's spread and an amortising loan's spread is not a constant: on a flat hazard curve it is ~0bp; on a realistically sloped one it is positive and grows with slope, because the loan's principal stays outstanding longer at the curve's riskier, far end. Worked example, same company, same 4-year WAL, bullet vs. an equal-principal amortiser to year 7:

hazard shapebulletamortiserwedge
flat (3%/yr throughout)182.7bp182.7bp ~0bp
rising (1%→5%/yr)119.4bp142.5bp +23.1bp

No single hard-coded markup is right across every curve shape — the only way to get the right number is to run the full survival-weighted sum again on the loan's own schedule. That re-run is what "repricing" means.

9. Recovery: why it is needed, and where it actually comes from

One observed bond price cannot separately reveal how likely default is and how severe it would be — spread ≈ hazard × (1 − recovery), one equation, two unknowns. Recovery has to be fixed to solve for hazard at all, and it is also a direct ingredient of the loan's own recovery leg. It is never observable for a live borrower — only published historical averages by seniority (Moody's/S&P recovery studies) or a realised number from an actual CDS default auction, which only exists after a company has already defaulted.

10. Floating-rate loans: margin is not automatically a z-spread

A loan quoted at Euribor-6M + 50bp is not yet in the same units as a bond's z-spread. Projecting each coupon off the curve's own forward rate for that period (not a flat, repeated spot rate) and solving for the equivalent flat spread handles the time-value piece; a genuinely different index tenor (3M vs. 6M Euribor) is a separate, quoted basis on top of that. See the Code tab for exactly where this is, and is not yet, wired in.

In the code — files, functions, what maps to what

Function by function, in the order the Tutorial built them up:

The curve: pb_curves.py

Curve.forward(t1, t2) is the whole of Tutorial step 2's "read the market's own forecast" in one line: log(df(t1) / df(t2)) / (t2 - t1) — the standard identity that recovers an implied forward rate purely from two discount factors, no separate projection model needed. bootstrap_curve() builds the curve this feeds from: it walks the quoted maturities in order and, at each one, calls solve_scalar (Brent's method, in pb_primitives.py) to find the one zero rate that makes that maturity's par swap worth exactly zero today, given every shorter zero rate already solved. Each maturity is pinned down using only information already fixed by the shorter ones — nothing is fitted jointly.

The valuation equation: pb_hazard.py

HazardCurve.cumulative(t) is Tutorial step 4's ∫λ written as an array operation: for each requested time it measures how much of each hazard "node width" has elapsed, clips that at the node's own span, and dot-products with the three levels — survival() then just takes exp(-cumulative).

risky_pv() is Tutorial step 5's equation, with nothing abstracted away:

PV = Σ DF(t) · [ S(t)·CF + R·(S(t−)−S(t))·Outstanding ]

— s_now/s_prev are S(t) and S(t−); default_leg is the recovery term, and it multiplies by sched.outstanding, never the original face — the function's own docstring calls using original notional here "a common and material error", which is precisely why an amortiser recovers less in absolute terms than a bullet: by the time it could default, less of it is left to recover. par_spread() re-runs that same equation but makes the coupon (forward_rate + s) — the forward projection from Tutorial step 10, reused rather than re-derived — and Brent-solves for the s that prices the whole thing back to par. This one function is simultaneously M0's final output and the mechanism behind "margin is not automatically a z-spread": the identical code path prices a par-floater's margin and reprices a loan's schedule.

Calibration: fit_hazard(), concretely

The residual vector handed to scipy.optimize.least_squares is, in order: one repricing error per admitted bond (risky_pv(...) - observed_price), then — only if a peer prior is supplied — a second block, sqrt(prior_weight) * (diff(fitted_levels) - diff(prior_levels)). That second block is Tutorial step 7 made literal: it penalises the shape (the first differences between adjacent hazard levels) departing from the peer curve's shape, and says nothing at all about the level, which is exactly the level/slope split the Tutorial argued for. The fit runs in log-space (x0 = log(prior_levels), levels recovered via exp(sol.x)) purely so hazards can never go negative without needing an explicit constraint.

shrinkage_weight(n, k=2.0) returns the simple n/(n+k) fraction; pb_m0_fairvalue.fair_value() turns that weight on the issuer's own slope into the penalty weight the residual block above actually needs via prior_weight = (1 - w_own) / w_own * len(prices) — a thin-curve issuer with a low w_own gets a large penalty weight, pulling its fitted shape hard toward its peers'; an issuer with many bonds gets a penalty near zero and its own data simply wins.

Why curve is a list, not one shared object

fit_hazard() accepts curve as either one shared Curve or a list, one per observation. That is not an API convenience; it is the only way to respect the Tutorial's claim that a hazard curve belongs to the issuer, never to one bond's currency. The same borrower's EUR and USD bonds are expected to imply the same default intensity, so each bond must be stripped against its own currency's discount curve before the resulting hazard readings are pooled into one fit — stripping every bond on one shared curve regardless of currency would quietly contaminate the hazard level with a currency-basis effect that has nothing to do with credit risk.

The uncertainty band: perturb and refit, not a formula

fair_value()'s band is not a closed-form confidence interval. It takes the fit's own rmse_price, shifts every admitted bond's price by ± that amount, reruns fit_hazard() from scratch on the shifted prices, and reprices the loan again — the resulting two numbers become p5/p95. Uncertainty here is "how much would the answer move if the evidence were this much noisier," made concrete by literally redoing the fit under that noise rather than by deriving a variance algebraically.

Orchestration: pb_m0_fairvalue.fair_value() and pb_m0_batch.py

_observation_price() is the hard branch the Tutorial's margin-vs-z-spread discussion needs: FIXED bonds are priced from a quoted YTM via price_from_ytm; FLOATING bonds go through z_spread_from_quoted_margin() — currently wired only here, into the bond-evidence path, not yet into converting our own quoted loan margins before they reach quotes.proposed_spread_bp. pb_m0_batch.py runs the whole book in two deliberately ordered passes: pass 1 fits every client with ≥3 distinct admitted maturities without shrinkage and medians the results by rating into a peer curve; pass 2 refits everyone, including those thick-curve clients, with shrinkage toward that peer curve. Doing it the other order — shrinking toward a prior built from already-shrunk fits — would let the prior feed on itself.

Open, logged, not yet fixed

comparability_rules.json's recovery_assumption (40%) is the one flat value passed into every fit_hazard() and every risky_pv()/par_spread() call above, for both the bonds and the loan; see the pending item in the Reference tab.

Validated by test_m0_repricing.py (hazard levels within 5bp, loan-equivalent spread within 8bp, wedge sign and magnitude checked directly) — results/m0_repricing.json.

GATEThe comparability gate
Reference — the short version

Before any fitting happens, every candidate bond observation is tested against six conditions, and admitted only if it passes all of them. Exactly one rejection code is assigned when it fails, from a closed list:

STALEthe quote is older than the staleness window
ILLIQUIDthe bid/ask is too wide, or nothing has traded recently
OFF_TENORinside its final year, or the admitted set does not span the loan's average life without extrapolating past the curve's own support
WRONG_SENIORITYsubordinated, secured, or carrying a guarantee that does not match the loan being priced
CURRENCY_PROXYa cross-currency substitute where the basis is too wide, or not observed at all
SINGLE_SOURCEtoo few admitted bonds to separate the issuer's credit level from one bond's own idiosyncratic pricing

The gate is tuned deliberately for precision over recall: admitting a bad comparable corrupts the fair-value anchor and biases the result tight, which is the dangerous direction; rejecting a good one only costs an honest "insufficient evidence" verdict, which is recoverable. Validated against 28 hand-written cases — reasoned from how bond comparables are actually screened, not generated from the same model that is being tested — with every threshold swept to show how many bonds and clients would flip if it moved.

Tutorial — built up from first principles

Two instruments from the same issuer can still be a bad comparable pair — comparability is about more than "same name."

What makes a bond admissible at all

Each of the six checks guards against one specific way a price can be meaningless as evidence for M0: too old and the market has moved past it (STALE); too thin a market and the quote is noise, not a price (ILLIQUID); too far from the loan's own maturity and using it means extrapolating the hazard curve past anything it was ever fitted to (OFF_TENOR); a different seniority, security or guarantee and the bond is pricing a different risk entirely, not this issuer's standalone credit (WRONG_SENIORITY); a different currency's bond standing in without a reliable cross-currency basis (CURRENCY_PROXY); or simply too few bonds to tell the issuer's own level apart from one bond's idiosyncratic pricing (SINGLE_SOURCE).

A real example of why WRONG_SENIORITY exists

Early in this leg's build, a parent-guaranteed bond was briefly admitted as a comparable for an unguaranteed loan. A parent guarantee makes a bond trade tighter than the subsidiary's own standalone risk warrants — so using it as evidence understated exactly the number the loan needed, and did so quietly, with no error raised anywhere. The admission rules were made asymmetric specifically so a guarantee can only ever be used where it genuinely matches, never as a stand-in.

Why precision beats recall here specifically

A bad bond wrongly admitted looks exactly like a normal data point once it's in the hazard fit — there is nothing downstream that will catch it. A good bond wrongly rejected just costs a visible, honest "insufficient evidence" verdict. One error is silent and corrupts the anchor; the other is loud and safe. Every threshold in this gate is set with that asymmetry in mind, not to maximise how many bonds get admitted.

Why OFF_TENOR is a set-level test, not a per-bond one

An earlier version rejected any bond more than a fixed distance from the loan's own maturity. That discarded real information at precisely the issuers with the fewest bonds — a 12-year bond is exactly what you want informing the far end of a curve for a 7-year-WAL loan, even though it is not itself close to 7 years. The rule now asks whether the admitted set as a whole spans the loan's maturity, not whether each bond individually sits near it.

In the code — files, functions, what maps to what

gate_observation(obs, target, rules) runs the six checks in exactly one order, and the order is part of the design, not an implementation detail:

  1. Required fields raise, they never default. The first six lines fetch seniority, guarantee, currency, maturity_date, obs_date and raise KeyError if any is missing — on both the observation and the target. The comment is explicit about why: a missing seniority that silently defaulted to SENIOR_UNSECURED would admit subordinated paper as if it were senior unsecured, "the single worst failure this gate can have." A loud crash here is strictly safer than a quiet, wrong admission.
  2. STALE is checked before ILLIQUID, deliberately. Liquidity is read off bid_ask_bp and days_since_trade — but those readings are themselves unreliable once a quote is old, so reporting ILLIQUID on a stale observation would be naming a number the gate does not trust as the reason for rejecting it.
  3. OFF_TENOR here is only a floor — resid < min_residual_maturity_years — because whether the admitted set as a whole spans the loan's WAL is a different, set-level question, answered separately by assess_sufficiency() once every bond has already been gated one at a time.
  4. Seniority and guarantee are two separate adjacency-matrix lookups: sn["admit_matrix"][target.seniority] lists which observed seniorities may stand in for this target, and guarantee_admit_matrix does the same for guarantee status. Keeping them as two matrices (rather than one combined rule) is what let the earlier parent-guarantee mistake get fixed by editing one matrix cell rather than rewriting the branch.
  5. Currency proxying has its own three-step fallback: a different currency is rejected outright unless cross-currency proxies are allowed; if allowed, a missing basis observation is rejected if the rules require one; if the basis exists, it is rejected only if it exceeds max_xccy_basis_bp. Three separate ways to fail, not one blanket check, so the rejection code's underlying reason stays specific.

assess_sufficiency() runs after every individual bond has already passed: first the plain count test (n_admitted < min_admitted_observations → SINGLE_SOURCE), then the set-level span test — target_wal must fall inside [min(admitted) - tolerance, max(admitted) + tolerance], replacing the earlier per-bond proximity rule the Tutorial described.

Both functions are called from pb_m0_fairvalue.fair_value() in that same two-stage order: every observation gated individually first, sufficiency assessed on what survives second — the file's own docstring states this as a design rule ("it never looks at more than one observation at a time, except for the client-level sufficiency test").

M1 · M2The clearing model
Reference — the short version

M0 answers what the borrower's bonds imply. A separate, harder question is where will a competing lender actually price this deal, split into two linked pieces fitted together as one hierarchical Bayesian model across the whole client book: M1, the loan-bond basis (how a loan typically prices relative to the same borrower's bond-implied fair value), and M2, the structural model of where a competitor would actually bid.

M2: outcomes are censored, not binary

A win tells us only that the best competing offer was above our price; a loss on price tells us only that it was below. The actual competing number is almost never observed. Both are treated as interval constraints on one latent quantity — the "clearing spread" — rather than as a win/loss label fed to a classifier, which discards exactly the information that makes the number informative.

M2: losses are split by reason, and this matters more than any other single modelling choice

A deal lost because a competitor undercut us says something about price. A deal lost because the borrower issued a bond instead, restructured the deal, or stayed with an incumbent lender says nothing about price at all. Pooling the two flattens the estimated price-sensitivity in direct proportion to how many non-price losses are in the sample — roughly a third of all losses in this book — and every naive win-rate model in the market makes exactly this error.

M1 and M2 both: sparse cells borrow strength from their cohort

With roughly 20 closed deals a year across 200 clients, 30 markets, 2 currencies and 5 tenor bands, most client-level cells hold at most one observation. Both the basis (M1) and the competitor level (M2) are pooled hierarchically — client, within sector×rating, within country, within region — so a thinly-covered name inherits its cohort's level rather than fitting noise to itself. How much of any given number is the client's own evidence versus its cohort's is reported, not hidden: see "Evidence strength" below.

M1: why the bond-implied fair value enters as an estimated quantity, not a fact

M0's output feeds M1 as a regressor, and M0 is itself an estimate with its own error. Treating it as exact data is a textbook statistical mistake (errors-in-variables) that attenuates the estimated loan-bond pass-through toward zero and makes every other coefficient near it overconfident. M1 carries M0's own reported uncertainty forward explicitly, which is what let the pass-through coefficient recover to 0.918 (true value 0.90) instead of settling, overconfidently, on 0.915.

M2: why a correction is needed at all

The quote we put on the table is not independent of what we believe about the market — we price tighter when we sense a competitive deal. Because of that, the censoring threshold itself depends on the thing being estimated, which is a textbook source of bias (endogeneity). We confirmed its size directly: rebuilding the identical synthetic world with a quote that does not depend on our own read of the market cuts the resulting bias from −16.5bp to −6.2bp — so roughly two-thirds of it is this specific mechanism. The 16.5bp correction seen throughout the application is added to M2's own estimate of the clearing level, not to any quote or any historical record, to undo that one-sided pull. The remedy that removes the cause rather than correcting for it — wider market-intelligence coverage, a valid instrument, or deliberate price variation inside committee tolerance — is a data-collection question for the desk, not a modelling one.

Tutorial — built up from first principles

Why a model beyond M0 is needed at all

The bond market and the loan market are not the same market. M1 models the basis level between them, not the level itself, because that basis is far more stable than either market's level alone — a 200bp market-wide move can leave the basis within a few bp of where it started. Modelling the stable thing is a large variance reduction, and it is what lets the model survive a regime shift the level-only model could not.

Partial pooling, with a plain analogy

Forecasting weather for a small town with ten years of its own records: lean heavily on the town's own history. For a town with one year of records: lean mostly on the region's climate instead, folding in the town's own single year as it accumulates more. Hierarchical pooling (client ← sector×rating ← country ← region ← global) does exactly this, automatically, for both M1's basis and M2's competitor level — the data itself determines how much weight each level earns.

Censoring, with a plain analogy

You don't know a stranger's exact height, only that they cleared a fence you can measure. A win says the competitor's price was above ours — taller than our fence; a loss on price says it was below. M2 treats every outcome as one of these one-sided bounds on a hidden number, s_comp, rather than forcing it into a win/loss label, which would throw away the "how much above or below" information these bounds still carry.

Competing risks, with a plain analogy

Estimating how price-sensitive customers are, but a third of the people who didn't buy walked away for reasons that had nothing to do with price — include them in a price-sensitivity estimate and the true effect of price gets diluted by noise that isn't about price at all. M2 excludes LOST_ON_OTHER from the price-response likelihood for exactly this reason.

Errors-in-variables, with a plain analogy

Regressing Y on a noisy measurement of X, instead of the true X, always flattens the fitted slope toward zero — the noise scatters the X-axis without correspondingly scattering Y, so the apparent relationship looks weaker than it is. M0's output is exactly this kind of noisy X inside M1; carrying its known uncertainty forward rather than treating it as exact is the standard fix.

Gibbs sampling, briefly

Rather than solve for every unknown parameter at once, a Gibbs sampler repeatedly draws each one from its own conditional distribution, holding the current guesses for all the others fixed, and cycles through all of them many times. Because the pieces here are conjugate (normal-on-normal, with the censored observations handled by an auxiliary truncated-draw step), each conditional draw is exact, not approximate — which is why no separate sampler (PyMC, NUTS) turned out to be necessary.

In the code — files, functions, what maps to what

A deliberate deviation from the original file plan: pb_basis_model.py (M1) and pb_price_response.py (M2) were never built as separate files. They share one coupled hierarchy and one sampler — splitting the files would have meant awkwardly splitting one estimation problem — so both live together in pb_posterior.fit(), one Gibbs sweep that cycles through seven blocks every iteration. Each block below is one piece of Tutorial intuition made literal:

Before the sampler ever runs: pb_calibrate.build_observations()

This is where the Tutorial's competing-risks exclusion actually happens — LOST_ON_OTHER and NO_DECISION rows are filtered out before they ever reach fit(), which is why the sampler itself contains no notion of "price-independent loss" at all; by the time it sees data, that noise is already gone. The function also sets each row's measurement-error scale from how the intel was sourced — INTEL_NOISE = {"FIRM_QUOTE": 10.0, "CLIENT_REPORTED": 25.0, "HEARSAY": 50.0} — and the oracle_b flag switches off the errors-in-variables treatment entirely (feeding M0's point estimate in as if exact), kept only so the attenuation it causes can be measured on purpose.

Step 1 — the censored latent, with label noise layered on top

_rtruncnorm() is the fence analogy as code: given a mean, an sd, and a [lo, hi] window, it draws from a normal truncated to that window — exactly a win or a loss, expressed as "somewhere above/below this line," not a point. Layered on top is the label-noise mixture: for every censored row the sweep computes how likely the recorded direction is versus its opposite (z_as, via the normal CDF), weights "as recorded" by (1−eps) and "flipped" by eps·(1−as-recorded), and actually swaps the bound direction for rows that draw into the flipped camp. A genuinely contradictory set of constraints (hi_r ≤ lo_r later, in step 5) is treated as evidence of exactly this kind of mislabelling, not as an error.

An absent bound is represented as ±1e6 in the data contract — a finite number, deliberately, so the bounds arrays stay plain floats with no None/NaN handling scattered through the sweep. Every place that reads a bound checks it against an explicit SENT = 1e5 threshold first (np.abs(lo0) < SENT) rather than using the raw value directly, which is what keeps a sentinel from ever being treated as a real, very wide, constraint.

Step 1b — B as a latent, not a regressor (errors-in-variables)

This block only runs where s_B (M0's reported uncertainty) is present. It draws a latent B_i from its two sources of information at once — the observed C (weighted by how strongly beta ties them together) and the noisy reading B_hat_i (weighted by 1/s_B_i²) — which is precisely Tutorial step 5's "carry the noisy X's own uncertainty forward" made into an extra conjugate draw rather than an approximation. B_hat_c is captured once, immediately after the design matrix's one centring step, and reused as the fixed reference point for every sweep from then on — the comment at that line notes explicitly that this value is already (B − mean), which is what stops a second, redundant centring from ever being applied to it later in the function. The draw is also re-anchored to B_hat_c[eiv].mean() every sweep: each individual B_i only has one reading from each side (one C, one B_hat), so it is weakly identified on its own, and re-anchoring the column's mean every sweep is what stops it drifting as a whole to chase the overall level instead of staying tied to what was actually observed.

Steps 2–3 — the pooling itself

_draw_normal_coeffs() is one conjugate multivariate-normal draw for the fixed-effect coefficients (entity type, currency, fixed/floating, appetite, size, and β — M1's pass-through). _group_means() plus the same conjugate form then draws the country and client random effects — this is the "lean on the cohort when your own history is short" mechanism: each group's posterior mean blends its own average residual against the global mean, weighted by how many observations that group actually has (c_ / sigma² versus 1/s_cty²).

u and v are re-centred to average zero after every draw (u -= average(u, weights=...)), which is required for identifiability, not optional tidying: a uniform shift in the random effects can always be exactly cancelled by an opposite shift in the intercepts, and nothing in the likelihood prefers one split over the other unless something pins the random effects down independently. The centring is observation-weighted, not a plain average, specifically because group sizes here range from 1 to 40 — an unweighted zero-mean constraint does not make the weighted mean zero when groups this unbalanced are involved, and it is the weighted mean, not the plain one, that the intercepts actually need held at zero.

Step 4 — the basis random walk

Each time knot's value is drawn conditional on its immediate neighbours only (a first-order Gaussian Markov chain), with rw_s0 = 1.2 pinned deliberately tight. The comment explains why: this walk and a market-wide "lender appetite" covariate both move with the calendar, so left loose, the walk quietly absorbs the covariate's own signal — the "two time terms fighting" risk named in the architecture doc, made concrete as a specific, named parameter rather than left as a general warning.

Step 5 — rho, derived from the same bounds that constrain C

This is the step that needs the intel channel, per the Reference tab's identification argument. Concretely: every outcome bound is first expressed as a constraint on rho itself (rho ≥ bound_lo − C, rho < bound_hi − C), intersected across a client's deals, then drawn as a truncated normal around the pooled rho_mu. Deriving these bounds from exactly the same lo0/hi0 array the censored likelihood itself uses, rather than re-deriving them separately from our quoted price, is what makes outright wins, price losses and last-look wins all handled the same way automatically: a last-look win's real constraint is on the matched price, not the original wider quote, and reading the bound off the same array C is already constrained by means there is only one place that number can come from, for every outcome type at once.

Steps 6–7 — variances and label noise

Every variance (sigma, s_cty, s_cli, s_rw) is drawn from a scaled inverse-chi-squared posterior anchored at a hand-set prior, never a flat prior — the comment is explicit that this is "domain knowledge... not a crutch," because with 86% of rows bounded on only one side, a flat prior on sigma has no fixed point: a large sigma produces wide latent draws, which produce large residuals, which keep sigma large. An earlier run with flat variance priors walked sigma to its clip at 60 against a truth of 12. The intel-selection offset (step 6b) is one more conjugate normal draw, directly implementing the Reference tab's correction for selectively reported deals; the label-noise rate eps (step 7) is a Beta draw counting how many censored rows got flipped this sweep versus how many stayed as recorded.

rhat_ess() computes split R-hat and effective sample size from the kept draws — the convergence check that says whether any of the above actually mixed.

Validated by test_parameter_recovery.py (coverage-based, every miss individually attributed) and test_endogenous_censoring.py (the counterfactual exogenous-quote rebuild behind the 16.5bp → 6.2bp finding).

M3The profitability floor
Reference — the short version

The minimum price reaching a 2% RoRWA hurdle, net of cost of funds, is given by the bank's existing profitability engine for every combination of borrower rating, sovereign rating, tenor band and currency — this leg does not model capital, RWA or expected loss, and treats that boundary as fixed by design. What this leg adds is a queryable, monotone surface over that grid (so results never run backwards as risk worsens) and the one property the raw matrix does not carry: its drift over time as the bank's own cost of funds moves, which is what makes the six-month quote-shelf-life question in the window monitor answerable at all.

Tutorial — built up from first principles

What a profitability floor means, in plain terms

Every loan ties up a slice of regulatory capital sized by its risk weight; a 2% RoRWA hurdle says that capital has to earn at least 2% a year. For a given risk weight and expected loss, there is therefore a minimum spread below which the deal cannot clear that bar. This leg never computes any of that machinery — capital, RWA and expected loss are entirely the profitability engine's job, consumed here as a finished grid.

Why monotonicity is a sanity check, not a modelling choice

If two deals are identical except one borrower is riskier, that borrower's floor can never be lower. A floor that runs backwards as risk worsens isn't a surprising market fact — it is a bug in the upstream grid, and this leg's job is to catch that, not to silently smooth over it.

Why the floor moves even when nothing about the borrower changes

The floor nets out the bank's own cost of funds, and that cost drifts with the market independent of the borrower's own risk. A quote that cleared the floor comfortably in March can breach it by October purely because funding costs moved — which is exactly the quantity the six-month window-monitor view needs to track.

In the code — files, functions, what maps to what

Reading the engine's grid

FloorSurface.__init__() turns the engine's columnar export (a flat list of cells plus a _columns header) into a plain dict keyed by the 4-axis tuple (internal_rating, country_rating, tenor_bucket, currency) — a one-time reshape so every later lookup is an O(1) dict access rather than a scan. One such table is kept per monthly version; version_for(month) picks the latest version dated on or before the month asked for, which is what makes query() a genuinely point-in-time lookup rather than always reading today's grid.

query(): nearest rating, interpolated tenor — and in that order

Rating lookups go through _nearest_rating(), which snaps to the closest rating in the table rather than interpolating between two — the Tutorial's point made literal: BBB and BB are ordinal categories, and a number halfway between them does not describe a credit quality the engine ever actually priced. Tenor, by contrast, genuinely is continuous, so query() pulls the floor at each of the five WAL buckets' midpoints, enforces monotonicity first — np.maximum.accumulate(vals), run before interpolating — and only then calls np.interp() for the requested WAL. Enforcing before interpolating, not after, is deliberate: an input glitch in the raw grid must not get smoothed into a plausible-looking but wrong interpolated value. The function also reports whether the requested WAL landed exactly on a grid point or had to be interpolated (meta["interpolated"]), so a memo never presents an interpolated floor as if it were a directly quoted one.

drift() and monotonicity_report()

drift() is nothing more than two query() calls at different months, subtracted — the floor's movement for an otherwise unchanged scenario, and the direct input to the window monitor's shelf-life question. monotonicity_report() walks all three ordinal axes using one small helper, _desc(seq), which simply counts how many times a sequence of floors goes down when the thing it's indexed by (rating, country rating, or tenor) is supposed to only ever make the deal riskier. Any non-zero count here is flagged as an upstream engine or ingest bug, never silently tolerated or smoothed away — the same report also counts cells where the engine's own stated implied_rorwa falls short of the 2% hurdle it was supposed to guarantee.

No capital or RWA logic is reimplemented anywhere in this leg — every function above is a consumer or interpolator of the grid the profitability engine returns, never a re-derivation of it.

M4From a distribution to a quote
Reference — the short version

Given the clearing-spread distribution and the floor, the decision layer solves

maximise   P(win at spread s) × (s − floor) × size × life    subject to   s ≥ floor

The floor is a hard constraint, never a soft penalty: it is never traded off against a higher win probability, by construction. The optimum is reported as a three-rung ladder — walk-away (the floor itself), recommended (where the product above peaks) and stretch (the widest spread still retaining a workable win probability) — because a negotiation needs an opening level and a floor, not only a single number. The unwinnable-band flag fires when the floor sits above the 90th percentile of the clearing distribution: not merely an expensive deal, one with no spread that is both compliant and competitive at any price.

Verified against 10,000 randomised posteriors: 0 floor breaches, 0 ladder-ordering violations, unwinnable-flag precision 100% and recall 100%. Integrating the optimisation over the full posterior rather than a single point estimate changes the recommended quote by 22.0bp on average — material, which is why the full integration is kept rather than the cheaper plug-in approximation.

Tutorial — built up from first principles

Why there is a genuine peak, not a corner solution

As the quoted spread rises, the win probability falls but the profit per deal rises — the product of the two has a real interior maximum, not a solution that just wants to go as high or as low as possible. That peak is the "recommended" rung.

Hard constraint vs. soft penalty, concretely

A hard floor constraint means the search never even considers a price below it, no matter how attractive its expected value looks on paper. A soft penalty just subtracts a cost for breaching the floor — and if that cost isn't large enough, a sufficiently profitable non-compliant price can still slip through. This system only ever uses the hard form.

Why the ladder has three rungs, not one

A negotiation needs an opening ask and a floor below which there is nothing left to give, not a single number. Walk-away is the floor itself; stretch is the widest price that still keeps a workable chance of winning — the two bookends a real conversation actually needs.

A real bug that shows why "just round" is fragile

Rounding the walk-away rung down to the nearest quoting increment pushed it through the floor in roughly half of randomised posteriors tested. Rounding up fixed that, but then the search grid — which still started at the unrounded floor — sometimes produced a "recommended" rung below the now-higher rounded walk-away rung, breaking the ladder's own ordering. The fix was to start the search grid at the rounded value, not just round the final answer — a small detail that broke the ladder's basic ordering guarantee until it was found.

Why integrate over the whole posterior, not just its mean

Plugging in only the mean of each uncertain input and then optimising is not the same as optimising first and averaging second, whenever the thing being optimised is a non-linear function of those inputs — which an expected-value product with a hard floor constraint certainly is. The two approaches were tested against each other directly and disagree by a measurable amount, which is why the costlier full integration is kept.

In the code — files, functions, what maps to what

Two small functions carry all the economics

economic_profit_bp() is (spread − floor) × notional × WAL: subtracting the floor first, before multiplying by size, is what makes a deal priced exactly at the floor worth zero by construction — the optimiser can never trade volume for value it does not actually have. win_curve() is a direct Monte Carlo estimate, not a formula: for each candidate spread on the grid it counts the fraction of the posterior's own (clearing + rho) draws that clear that spread — (eff ≥ s).mean() — so the win-probability curve is literally "how often would we have won, across every plausible world the posterior considers." Adding rho before comparing is what separates "the market cleared here" from "we personally win here": a client with a real non-price advantage can sit wide of the best competitor and still clear.

decide(), the solve itself

Before anything else, if the config says so, the posterior's clearing draws get the measured clearing_bias_correction_bp added on — the same +16.5bp correction from the M1·M2 card, applied here rather than at the source, so raw historical records are never touched. The walk-away rung is then rounded up to the quoting increment, never down — rounding toward the floor could in principle land a fraction of an increment inside it, and the floor is a hard constraint a desk should never quote through, not even by a rounding convention — and the search grid is built to start exactly at that rounded value, which is what guarantees walk_away ≤ recommended by construction rather than by coincidence: both the rounding direction and the grid's own starting point have to agree for the ladder's ordering to hold.

Expected value at every grid point is simply win_curve × economic_profit, and recommended is wherever that product peaks (argmax) — the genuine interior optimum the Tutorial described, found by direct search over the grid rather than solved analytically, because nothing about the shape of pw × prof guarantees a closed form. stretch is the widest grid point whose own win probability still clears stretch_win_probability, floored at recommended so the ladder can never report out of order. unwinnable is one comparison — floor_bp > percentile(clearing_draws, 90) — and plugin=True collapses every draw to its own median before any of the above runs, which is precisely how validate_decision_layer.py isolates what integrating over the full posterior actually buys versus optimising a single point estimate.

Classification lives in a different function on purpose

pb_verdict.pricing_verdict() computes INSIDE/AT/OUTSIDE_MARKET purely from where our_spread_bp sits against the clearing posterior's median, independent of the floor entirely; recommended_action() is a short, explicit decision tree (NO_RELIABLE_BENCHMARK → ESCALATE; unwinnable → WALK; too little evidence → ESCALATE; recommended below floor → WALK; win probability already high at the ceiling → STRETCH; otherwise QUOTE) that never touches the verdict variable except to read it. The two can and do disagree — inside market but walk when the floor binds regardless of how the market looks, outside market but quote when there's a documented strategic reason — and that disagreement is a feature the schema was built to allow, not an inconsistency to resolve.

ev_per_banker_hour() is still in the code, its own docstring now marked deprecated: it was theoretically defensible (attention is the binding constraint, so attention belongs in the denominator) but failed in practice because no reader could verify the denominator, and a ranking nobody trusts reallocates nothing. The views now rank on plain euro expected value and show effort separately.

Validated by validate_decision_layer.py (10,000 randomised posteriors; the floor-violation and ladder-ordering counts quoted in the Reference tab).

M5The forward view and the window monitor
Reference — the short version

A quote made today is judged by the borrower months later. Holding a price for six months is, in substance, granting the borrower a free option on credit spreads — export credit agencies already price exactly this through CIRR holding periods; commercial lenders typically do not.

What is forecast, and what deliberately is not

Interest rates are read from the forward curve, never forecast. The forward curve is the market's own expectation of where rates will be, it costs nothing to use, and presenting a proprietary rate view on top of it would be model risk with no offsetting benefit.

Only two things are forecast, because only two are genuinely specific to this bank rather than observable in the market: our own cost of funds (mean-reverting to its trailing 12-month average) and a two-factor model of systemic credit conditions fitted to the iTraxx / CDX / MOVE / VIX panel — two principal components, not three, because a third component on roughly five years of monthly data would fit noise rather than signal. Both are simulated forward hundreds of times to produce a distribution, not a single path.

The headline output is a probability, not a forecast

Credit spreads are close to a random walk at a six-month horizon, so a point prediction adds little on its own. The output that matters is P(a deal exists that is both compliant and competitive) — the clearing distribution and the floor, evaluated jointly, because the two move with overlapping drivers and treating them as independent would overstate how often a window is genuinely open.

Tutorial — built up from first principles

Why read the forward curve instead of forecasting rates

The forward curve already is the market's collective best guess, priced in by arbitrage: if enough people believed a different path was more likely, they would trade on that belief until the forward curve itself moved to match it. Proposing a different, in-house rate view on top of that is pure model risk with no edge to justify it — the discipline is to read the forward curve, never to second-guess it.

What a factor model is, intuitively

Dozens of credit-market series — iTraxx, CDX, sector sub-indices, sovereign CDS, MOVE, VIX — mostly move together on "risk-on / risk-off" days. Compressing them into two common drivers (two principal components) captures almost all of that shared movement without pretending each of thirty series needs its own independent model. A third component, on roughly five years of monthly data, would be fitting noise rather than a real, repeatable pattern — a concrete instance of the general rule that more parameters than the data can support make a model worse, not better.

Mean reversion and half-life, in one picture

A quantity that drifts back toward its own long-run average after a shock is mean-reverting; its half-life — the time for a shock to decay halfway back — is the single most intuitive number for communicating how fast that happens, far more so than the underlying decay-rate parameter itself.

Why simulate hundreds of paths rather than report one number

What matters for the window monitor is the shape of the distribution of future outcomes — how often the window stays open — not a single best-guess path, especially at a six-month horizon where spreads behave close to a random walk and a point forecast carries very little information on its own.

In the code — files, functions, what maps to what

Finding the two factors: fit_ar1_factors()

The function centres the whole credit-indicator panel (iTraxx, CDX, sovereign CDS, MOVE, VIX and the rest) and runs one SVD on it — a principal-component decomposition done the direct linear-algebra way, not fitted iteratively. The first n_pc=2 components become the factors; each factor's own AR(1) coefficient is then estimated with nothing more than a lag-1 regression (dot(f[:-1], f[1:]) / dot(f[:-1], f[:-1]) — literally "how much does tomorrow look like a scaled copy of today," the simplest possible estimator of mean-reversion speed), clipped to stay inside (−0.99, 0.99) so the walk can never explode or reverse. Why exactly two: explained reports each component's share of total variance, and the code simply never asks for a third — the Tutorial's parsimony argument (a third component on ~five years of monthly data fits noise) is enforced by never fitting it, not by fitting it and discarding it.

simulate_factors() and forecast_cof(): the same mean-reversion idea, run forward

Both functions share one simple recursion — next = mean + phi × (current − mean) + noise — applied horizon times to n=2000 independent starting copies at once, which is what turns a single fitted AR(1) model into an actual distribution of future paths rather than one projected line. forecast_cof() applies the identical recursion to the bank's own cost-of-funds series specifically, reverting to its own trailing 12-month average with an innovation size taken straight from the realised month-to-month changes.

The headline number is a comparison, not a forecast

window_probability() is the same Monte Carlo counting trick as M4's win_curve(), reused: (clearing_draws ≥ floor_draws).mean(), counted pairwise across the same simulated paths rather than against each distribution's own marginal percentiles — which is exactly what makes it a joint, not independent, evaluation. classify() is a two-line threshold rule on how much that probability moves between today (p0) and six months out (p6); NARROWING specifically means a currently open window is forecast to move toward closed, which the function singles out as the highest-priority case because it is the one state where today's number is actively misleading about what to do next.

shelf_life_days(): censoring, again, in a new place

For every simulated path it finds the first month where the clearing draw falls below the floor draw (breach.argmax(axis=1), the index of the first True in a boolean array) and converts that to days. Paths that never breach inside the horizon keep the full horizon length as their "first breach" — the function's own docstring is explicit that this must be read as right-censored, not as "safe forever," which is the same censoring idea from the M1·M2 card showing up again, this time in the forward simulation rather than in historical win/loss records.

window_scenario_range(): deliberately never one number

For each path it computes give = max(clearing_at_horizon − clearing_today, 0) — the spread forgone if the market widens while the quote sits fixed — then reports the 25th/50th/75th percentile of give across all simulated paths, never a single collapsed figure. The docstring states the reasoning directly: a Black-style single option value computed from a latent spread estimated off roughly 100 historical deals is spurious precision, and reporting one number would invite exactly the false confidence this whole system exists to avoid.

VALIDATIONHow this is validated, including what did not work
Reference — the short version

Every figure below is measured, not asserted, and negative results are kept on the record rather than quietly dropped.

Parameter recovery

Against known truth on synthetic data with every adversarial feature the real world is expected to contain (censoring, selective reporting, mean-reverting label noise, correlated ratings, thin coverage): 87% of 90% credible intervals contain the true value, against an 85% target that is deliberately left unmet rather than lowered to match. Every miss is individually attributed to a named, understood cause; the harness fails only on misses that are not attributed, and currently there are none.

Calibration

Reliability is scored ahead of raw discrimination throughout: a model that ranks deals correctly while being systematically overconfident produces a confident, wrong quote ladder, and the ladder is the product. On a held-out test window, the raw predictive distribution's 90% interval covered 88.2% of outcomes (nominal 90%). Attempting to correct this with distributional recalibration — fitted on an adjacent time window — moved coverage to 82.7% this run. The sign of that move is not stable, and that instability is itself the finding. An earlier run of this same check, on a different random realisation of the identical adversarial DGP, measured raw coverage at 85.8% moving to 80.0% after recalibration — a materially worse fit, with opposite-skewed PIT histograms between the calibration and test windows. Recalibration assumes the model is wrong in the same way in both windows; a credit cycle does not guarantee that, and whether the assumption happens to hold varies run to run rather than failing consistently in one direction. A fix whose benefit depends on which realisation you happened to measure is not a fix to rely on. Built, tested twice, found unstable, and left switched off — recorded as a negative result rather than discarded quietly.

What is known not to work yet

  • The forward factor used for the window monitor is mean-reverting on synthetic data, so almost nothing registers as a window "narrowing" in testing. That is a property of the test data, not a validated finding about the real book, and the narrowing count should be read as untested until this runs on real history.
  • A systemic market-wide driver (lender appetite) and the basis random-walk term can both absorb the same movement in the data; the model is constrained to prevent this but the constraint trades away some of the appetite effect's own signal.
  • Label noise in committee records is under-detected at this sample size: with most outcomes bounding the clearing spread from one side only, a mislabelled loss reason is usually still consistent with the data.
  • Recovery is one flat number for both the comparable bonds and the loan being priced (M0), with no stress test yet for what happens when their seniority genuinely differs — our loans are typically senior secured, the comparable bonds typically senior unsecured. Tutorial — built up from first principles

What CRPS rewards, that a plain error score does not

A score like RMSE only checks whether a point forecast was close. CRPS scores a whole distribution: it rewards being both well-centred and appropriately sharp, and penalises a forecast that is either overconfident (too narrow, wrong often) or needlessly vague (too wide, uninformative). The deliverable here is a band, not a point, so the headline score has to judge the band.

Calibration vs. discrimination

A model can rank deals perfectly — correctly sorting which ones are more competitive than others (good discrimination, high AUC) — while being systematically overconfident about the actual numbers (bad calibration). A well-calibrated 30% win-probability prediction wins about 30% of the time; an overconfident one does not, even if its ranking of deals was right all along. A confidently wrong quote ladder is dangerous in a way a merely imprecise one is not, which is why calibration is scored ahead of raw discrimination throughout.

Why time-ordered folds, never random ones

A random fold can let the model "see" information from after some of its training points in spirit, because nearby time periods share the same regime. That specifically leaks the credit cycle — the one thing the model most needs to be tested against, not allowed to memorise. A time-ordered fold only ever trains on what would genuinely have been known at the time, which is the only honest test of "would this have worked."

The three baselines, and why they are not a straw man

Each baseline is what a skeptical managing director would propose in the first meeting: just use the last comparable deal; just take the bond spread and subtract a fixed typical basis; just add a fixed margin to the floor. All three are genuinely plausible shortcuts, which is exactly why the real model has to beat them on a fair, identical, time-ordered test rather than being compared only against itself.

Why coverage, not point accuracy, judges parameter recovery

A weather forecaster who says "30% chance of rain" is not wrong just because it rained — what matters, checked over many such forecasts, is whether it actually rains about 30% of the time when they say 30%. Parameter recovery here is judged the same way: not whether any single draw hit the true value exactly, but whether the stated 90% interval contains the truth about 90% of the time across many draws.

In the code — files, functions, what maps to what

pb_metrics.py: the scoring functions themselves

crps_ensemble(draws, y) computes the CRPS Tutorial intuition — reward both centring and sharpness — from its textbook definition, E|X−y| − 0.5·E|X−X'|, but the second term is not computed by the obvious pairwise loop over all draw pairs (which would be O(n²)); it uses the closed-form sorted-sample identity 2·Σ(2i−n−1)·x_i / n² on the sorted draws, giving the exact same number in O(n). The function's own comment notes it collapses to mean absolute error for a point forecast — so a model can only win by being sharper and correctly centred, never by one at the expense of the other.

pit_values(draws_matrix, y) is the forecast's own CDF evaluated at what actually happened, for every deal: (draws ≤ outcome).mean(), one number per deal. pit_uniformity() then checks whether those numbers, pooled across every deal, look uniform on [0,1] — both a chi-square test on a 10-bin histogram and a Kolmogorov–Smirnov test against the uniform distribution. This is the Tutorial's calibration idea turned into a single diagnostic: a model whose 90% intervals really do contain the truth 90% of the time produces PIT values that look like noise; one that is overconfident piles PIT values near 0 and 1 (outcomes keep landing outside a too-narrow band), and one that is underconfident piles them in the middle.

The test scripts: how each headline number in the Reference tab is actually produced

test_parameter_recovery.py does not just compute one coverage percentage and stop — every individual parameter whose 90% interval misses the known truth is looked up against a hand-maintained dictionary of named, understood causes (thin country-level cells, the deliberately pinned basis random walk, and so on). The harness's actual pass condition is stronger than "85% coverage": it is "85% coverage, or every single miss below that bar is one of the misses we already understand" — which is why the Reference tab can say every current miss is attributed rather than merely report a percentage.

validate_backtest.py builds the three baselines (pb_baselines.py's B1LastComparable, B2BondMinusBasis, B3FloorPlusMargin) behind the exact same fit() / predict_draws() interface the real model uses, so the walk-forward loop that calls one calls all four identically — no baseline gets an easier harness than the model it is being compared against. Each of the five folds trains only on deals dated before that fold's window, which is what makes the comparison honest about the regime shift rather than averaging over it.

validate_calibration.py is where the recalibration instability was actually measured: pb_metrics.fit_recalibrator() learns a correction on one time window and recalibrated_coverage() applies it to the next, and the result — which direction coverage moves — is stored fresh in results/calibration_report.json every time this is rerun. It moved the wrong way on one random realisation of the DGP and a small amount the right way on another; conformal_prediction: false in pb_priors.json reflects that the sign itself is not trustworthy, not a single bad run. The functions themselves were not deleted after the finding; only their switch was flipped, so it stays reproducible rather than becoming a sentence with no code behind it.

validate_provenance.py plants one deliberately mislabelled client_safe record and checks that the provenance layer actually refuses to let it through — a leak test that can fail is the only kind worth having; a check that always passes because nothing in the fixture could ever trip it would not be testing anything.
