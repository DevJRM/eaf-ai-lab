4Where the benefit comes from

Two effects: the loan's timing, and how your curve is quoted

The price you pay and the bond-equivalent price differ in two ways at once. They describe different instruments, a -year bond against this -year amortising profile, and they are priced different ways, a reading of your quoted curve against a period-by-period valuation. A third price separates the two: the reference bond, a -year bullet bond valued on your curve exactly as the loan is. Each step from one price to the next then changes one thing only.

8Beyond the price

A smoother debt profile, and a structure you can shape

Same average maturity, no redemption peak

The loan's redemptions fall, on average, years from signing, so in your average time to maturity it counts much like a -year bond. But a bond redeems in a single year, while this loan spreads repayment over instalments of , of the amount each, out to year . Your redemption profile gains length without a new peak, and refinancing risk is spread rather than concentrated.

A bond of the same average life puts on one date, year . This loan spreads repayment over instalments of , of the amount each, out to year . It removes a concentration point from your maturity profile rather than adding one, which supports liquidity planning and the maturity-profile assessment of rating agencies.

A complement to your issuance programme

One facility, priced at signing, diversifies your funding sources and investor base beyond your auction and syndication programme, with certainty of execution whatever market conditions bring. No new-issue concession or syndication fees apply; the loan's own fees apply instead (see the net benefit).

One facility, priced once

A single facility, priced at signing, replaces the several bond issues and market windows the same profile would otherwise need, with one set of documentation. Bond issuance costs and any new-issue premium fall away; the loan's own fees apply instead (see the net benefit).

How the curve is read matters

Maturities without a quote are filled by straight lines between the nearest quotes, and the average-life price depends on that interpolation. A fitted issuer curve (Bloomberg BVAL or a curve-finder fit) describes par bonds at every whole-year maturity and is the cleanest input. With quotes taken bond by bond, bonds trading far from par can shift each level by a few basis points.

10Questions you may have

The points we would challenge, if we were you

Why not simply issue a -year bond at the same price?

You can, and on price the two are level. The difference is the profile: the bond concentrates refinancing on one date, while the loan amortises over instalments to year . Replicating the loan's profile in your bond market, with a bond maturing on each repayment date, would cost , the bond-equivalent price, rather than .

If the price is below my bond-equivalent price, why would a lender offer it?

Average-life pricing is a widely used, transparent convention for amortising loans, and a lender's economics are set by its own funding, capital and relationship considerations rather than by your bond curve. This analysis measures what the convention is worth to you on your own curve; it makes no statement about the lender's margin.

Is the benefit real money, or a modelling result?

It is the present value of paying less than your own market price for the later periods of the loan, computed from your quoted bonds only. It is not a payment: it shows up as interest below the bond-equivalent level in the later years, offset in part by interest above it in the early years (section 6). The assumptions that cannot be observed in your bond prices move it by no more than .

A Z-spread is not my floating funding cost. Does the comparison hold?

The analysis does not use the Z-spread as a margin. A Z-spread is a measure of price: the spread that, added to the swap curve's zero rates, discounts your bonds' cash flows to their market prices. From your Z-spreads the analysis infers the cost of lending to you in each period, and prices the loan's margin on that basis. Only the average-life price charges the Z-spread itself as a margin; the cost of that convention, , is already netted in the benefit (section 4).

Why does the cost per period step up and down?

Each year's cost is inferred from the difference between neighbouring quoted levels. With a limited number of quotes joined by straight lines, those differences jump where the slope of the interpolated curve changes. A fitted issuer curve gives a smoother profile. The bond-equivalent price is a weighted average over all of the loan's periods, so a step in any single year carries a small weight in it.

What if my curve flattens after signing?

The price is fixed at signing, and the benefit shown is measured on the curve at signing. If your curve later flattened, new long-dated issuance would become cheaper for you, which would reduce, in hindsight, the value of having fixed the long end of this loan at today's levels. The analysis does not forecast your curve.

What if we prepay?

The benefit is back-loaded: it is positive in present value only from about year . The table in section 6 shows the position at earlier dates. Prepayment terms should be read with this in mind.

How do fees change the picture?

The headline excludes fees on both sides. As a yardstick, the benefit is equivalent to an upfront fee of on the loan amount: loan fees below that level leave you ahead of the bond-equivalent price, before counting the bond route's own issuance costs and any new-issue premium.

Does the recovery assumption drive the result?

No. Recovery is not observable in bond prices, so we test it: at 20% or 60% instead of the benefit moves by no more than (section 9). A different recovery changes the inferred default risk and the loss given default in opposite directions, which largely offset.

How does this compare with our auction or syndication cost?

The comparison uses your bonds at their secondary-market levels on Bloomberg YAS. Issuing new bonds usually adds a new-issue concession and, for syndications, fees; both are excluded here, so the bond route's all-in cost would be higher than the bond-equivalent price shown. The loan's own fees are excluded too, and section 7 gives the yardstick for comparing them.

How does this affect our ratings or covenants?

This analysis is economic only: it compares the cost of the loan with the cost of the same profile in your bond market. Accounting, tax, rating and covenant treatment depend on your circumstances and the final documentation, and are for your advisers.

Can the figures be reproduced on our own data?

Yes. The calculation needs one Bloomberg YAS snapshot of your bonds (Z-spreads and yields by maturity), the swap curve YAS measures them against, and the loan's profile. It runs in a spreadsheet without macros, and it checks itself: every quoted bond is repriced exactly, and the Z-spreads and yields must describe the same bonds (section 11).

11Methodology

How the figures are derived

The analysis uses a standard credit valuation, of the kind used to value credit default swaps and to build issuer curves. Your bond curve is the only source of credit information: from it we infer the cost of lending to you in each individual year, and price the loan period by period on that basis.

Market inputs
    Steps
    1. Build risk-free discount factors from the swap curve.
    2. Find, year by year, the default intensity that reprices each of your bonds exactly at its quoted level. This turns each quoted level, an average to maturity, into the cost of each individual year.
    3. Build the loan's half-yearly balance from its drawdown and repayment schedule.
    4. Price each six-month period at the cost your curve implies for it, weighted by the balance outstanding, the discount factor and the probability of no default. The weighted average is the bond-equivalent price of the whole profile.
    5. Compare it with the average-life price, your curve read at the loan's average life. The difference, multiplied by the loan's credit-adjusted annuity of years and the amount, is the present value of the benefit.
    The calculation, precisely

    From your quotes to the cost of each year. Each quoted maturity T is read as your par bond paying a coupon cT at its own frequency m. For the fixed-rate loan cT is the YAS yield. For the floating-rate loan cT is recovered from the YAS Z-spread z, which Bloomberg defines as a spread added to every swap zero rate:

    cT = m·(1 − DFz(T)) / Σ DFz(t),   DFz(t) = (1 + (zero(t) + z)/m)−m·t

    A default intensity λ for each year is then solved, year by year in closed form, so that each par bond is repriced exactly with recovery R paid on default. Survival to each date follows as S(t) = exp(−Σλ·Δt).

    The cost of each six-month period i (accrual a = 0.5, survival ratio pi = Si/Si−1, risk-free forward fi):

    si = (1 − pi)·(1 + fi·a − R) / (pi·a);   floating ki = si,   fixed ki = fi + si

    Bond-equivalent price and benefit, with balance Oi, discount factor DFi and amount N:

    wi = DFi·Si·a·Oi;   fair = Σ wi·ki / Σ wi;   PV = N·(fair − charged)·Σ wi

    Σ wi = years is the loan's credit-adjusted annuity. The bond-equivalent price, fair above, is the rate at which the loan's cash flows, valued on your curve, are worth exactly what is drawn: the price of funding this profile at your own bond curve.

    Data and built-in checks
    • All market inputs come from one Bloomberg snapshot: your YAS Z-spreads and yields by maturity, the swap curve YAS measures the Z-spreads against, and the 6-month fixing.
    • By construction, every quoted bond is repriced exactly by the inferred default intensities.
    • Consistency: the yields implied by your Z-spreads must equal your quoted yields at the maturities quoted for both. On this data the largest difference is ; above 1bp the data are flagged for review.
    • The calculation is mirrored in an independent implementation, and both agree to rounding.
    Points to note
      Limitations
      • Your bonds must be in the currency of the loan; the analysis makes no cross-currency adjustment.
      • The loan is modelled in six-month periods with an accrual of half a year; the loan's actual day count changes the result only marginally.
