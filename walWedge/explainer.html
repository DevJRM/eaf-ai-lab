The WAL Wedge in ten minutes

When we price an amortising loan off a borrower's bond curve, we read that curve at one point: the loan's average life. When the curve rises, that single reading is lower than what the borrower's own bonds say this loan is worth. The difference is the WAL wedge: a benefit to the borrower that we can measure, explain and use when we present the loan.

1 · THE HABIT

One price, read at the loan's average life

A borrower can raise money by issuing bonds or by taking an amortising loan. To price the loan we look at the borrower's own bonds, because their prices are the market's live view of its credit. A bond curve, though, gives a different spreadyield for every maturity, and our loan is not one maturity: here it is drawn in two halves over the first year and repaid in equal instalments up to year .

The usual habit is to summarise the loan by its weighted average life, years, and charge the curve at that point: over EURIBORa fixed rate of (the bond yield at years, restated for half-yearly payments). It sounds neutral, since the loan behaves "on average" like a -year bond. It is not neutral, and the next section shows why.

2 · WHY IT MISSES

Two ideas, both about time

Idea 1
A quoted spreadyield is an average

Drive ten hours at an average of 42 km/h and you may have crawled through traffic early and raced at the end. A bond's quote works the same way: the 10-year spreadyield, , is the average credit costrate of years 1 to 10. Peeled apart, year 10 on its own costs about . On a rising curve each late year costs more than the quote suggests.

Idea 2
The same average life is not the same timing

The average life measures how much money is lent and for how long, but not when. A -year bond keeps all the money out to year ; our loan keeps less of it out for much longer. Both have the same average life, yet the bond's money sits on average years out and the loan's years. The loan reaches late years that a bond of the same average life never sees.

Put together: the loan's money sits later than a -year bond's, and on a rising curve later money is dearer. One reading at the average life therefore charges less than the borrower's own curve says this loan is worth. Before the average life the borrower pays a little too much; after it, a lot too little.

3 · HOW WE MEASURE IT

The loan as a strip of mini-bonds

To put a number on it we need a yardstick, and the borrower already has one: its own bonds. Picture the loan as mini-bonds, one per instalment, each repaid on its own date and each priced on the borrower's curve at its own maturity. Funding the loan this way would cost the average of those prices, weighted by how much each mini-bond counts (its size, how long it runs, discounting and the chance of being repaid). That average is the bond-equivalent price, . The loan charges on every mini-bond; the gap, a year on the balance outstanding, is the benefit.

The comparison
Each mini-bond's own price against the one price the loan charges

Short mini-bonds (slate) cost less on the borrower's curve than the price charged; long ones (maroon) cost more. The long ones count most, so the average lands above the price charged.

Where the benefit comes from
Two effects, separated by a stepping stone: a -year bond priced the same way

The timing effect is the wedge proper: same average life, same valuation, only the shape of the loan differs. The quote-convention effect is small and would arise even for a bullet: a Bloomberg Z-spread is quoted as a discount rate, while a loan pays a margin.the bond's annual yield is restated for the loan's half-yearly payments. A fixed rate also contains the swap rate, so the same benefit can be split another way: a credit effect of and an interest-rate effect of , from the shape of the swap curve.

In money: a year on is worth today, or added up over the life without discounting. Lead with the first; the second is only for reference.

4 · WHEN IT ARRIVES

The benefit is back-loaded

The gain comes from the late years, so it arrives late. Counted in today's money, the borrower is behind for the first years, furthest behind around year (), back to even in year and ahead from then on. The full benefit is collected only by keeping the loan; repaid early, it can leave the borrower worse off. Say this up front: it is the first thing a careful treasurer will find.

5 · SPOTTING A CASE

What makes it large, small or negative

The answer depends on one thing above all: how much the borrower's curve rises between the loan's average life and its final maturity. Recovery rates and the level of the swap curve barely matter. 6 · TALKING TO A BORROWER

Say it in their words, and say the catch first

1Three things to say early
"We compare this loan with your own bonds."The yardstick is what the same money would cost the borrower if it issued its own bonds instead: one bond for each repayment, maturing on that repayment's date (the mini-bonds of section 3). Their average cost is the bond-equivalent price.
"The benefit builds up late, and needs a rising curve."It accrues after the average life, from about year , and shrinks or reverses if the curve flattens or inverts. Saying it first builds trust.
"Worth today, a year."Lead with value today and bp a year; undiscounted totals add up money from different years.
2Words to use, and to avoid
Use

Implied default probabilities, our margin or our funding cost
3Three questions you will hear
"Where is the catch?"There is none hidden, but there are conditions: the benefit needs a rising curve and arrives late, from about year . If the loan is repaid early it may not arrive at all.
"What if our curve changes before signing?"The benefit is measured on the curve at signing. Flatter makes it smaller, inverted reverses it; the Simulator shows how it moved in past market episodes.
"Why not just issue bonds?"It need not be either one or the other. The loan avoids a single large repayment date and is priced once for the whole profile, so it complements a bond programme rather than replacing it.

Glossary: the words on this page, and their names in the tutorial
Average life (WAL)
Average repayment date minus average drawing date: how much money is lent, and for how long. years here.
Average-life price
The borrower's curve read at the average life. The tutorial calls it the shortcut.
Price we charge
The average-life price plus any agreed margin above or below it (zero here). In client documents: price you pay.
Bond-equivalent price
What the borrower's own bonds imply for this exact loan, mini-bond by mini-bond. The tutorial calls it the fair price.
Reference bond
A bullet bond maturing at the average life, valued the same way as the loan. The tutorial calls it the reference bullet.
Benefit (the wedge)
Bond-equivalent price minus the price we charge. Positive: the borrower pays less than its bonds imply.
Timing / quote-convention effect
The benefit split by cause: the loan's shape against the bond's, and the way the bond is quoted against the way the loan pays.
Credit / interest-rate effect
For a fixed-rate loan, the benefit split into its credit part and its swap-curve part.
Z-spread and yield (YAS)
Bloomberg's quotes of the borrower's bonds, maturity by maturity: the spread over the swap curve (for a floating-rate loan) and the all-in yield (for a fixed-rate loan).
Value today
The yearly benefit over the loan's life, discounted and allowing for the chance of default.
