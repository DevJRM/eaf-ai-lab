Where the wedge pays: which borrowers to call first

The WAL wedge is worth most to a borrower whose own bond curve keeps rising past the loan's average life. We read the curves of sovereigns and investment-grade companies around the world, as they stand in November 2026, to find where that is true today and how long it is likely to last. In short: the broad market is an ordinary moment for the wedge, but a handful of borrowers have curves as steep as anything seen in ten years. Most of them are in central and eastern Europe, Indonesia, the Philippines and Mexico.

1 · THE ANSWER

Five places to start, in order

Ranked on the five tests of section 2 together: how steep the borrower's curve is past the average life, whether the curve can be measured and is free of stress, and how winnable the argument is (borrowers for whom a matching bond is hard to issue, rather than treasuries that already hold a competitor's quote).

    Hold back on US and European investment-grade companies: their credit curves flatten past 7–10 years, and their treasuries compare loans with their own bond curves daily, so the playbook (§9) says price to clear rather than argue. Euro area governments now show steep curves against swaps, but mostly because long government yields sit above swaps, and they fund in bonds anyway: their agencies, regions and state companies are the way in. France is steep because it is under stress, which can turn quickly.

    2 · WHAT WE LOOK FOR

    Five tests, taken from the explainer and the playbook

    1 · RiseThe borrower's curve climbs between the loan's average life and its final maturity. The more it climbs, the larger the wedge: roughly the rise in bp per year × the average life ÷ 3.
    2 · DirectionIs the curve getting steeper or flatter? A wedge measured at signing is fixed, but a curve that is flattening may not be there by the time the loan is signed.
    3 · No stressWhen a borrower is in trouble its short bonds can yield more than its long ones (an inverted curve). Then the borrower pays more than its bonds imply: do not pitch.
    4 · MeasurableThe borrower has bonds across several maturities, so one Bloomberg snapshot gives a full curve. Without one, the benefit can be asserted but not shown.
    5 · WinnableOur playbook: the argument wins where a long amortising bond is hard to issue, and loses against a liquid-curve treasury with a competitor's quote in hand.

    Floating-rate loans depend only on the credit curve (spread over swaps). Fixed-rate loans also pick up the slope of the swap curve itself between the average life and the final maturity; today that adds only 3.0–5.4bp a year to a 13-year loan (section 4).

    3 · IS NOW A GOOD MOMENT?

    An ordinary moment for the market, an unusual one for a few borrowers

    Each line is the WAL Wedge engine (the Python copy of the Calculator) run once a month since January 2016 on real curves, for the two borrower groups with a full public curve history at every maturity: euro area governments (ECB par curve of all euro area governments) and US high-grade companies (US Treasury HQM corporate curve, rated AA to A). No single tenor is used: each month the whole curve from 1 to 30 years goes in, the price we charge is read at the loan's average life (7 years), and the bond-equivalent price uses every half-year out to the final maturity (13 years). The loan is the same every month, so the lines show only how the curves changed.

    One limit: there is no free ten-year history of swap curves. The history therefore measures against government curves (ECB AAA curve, Treasuries). The fixed-rate line is reliable on that basis, because a fixed-rate loan is priced off the borrower's all-in yields, which are real (today 13.0bp against government curves, 13.8bp against EURIBOR swaps). The floating-rate line is the credit spread over government curves; against swaps, as YAS measures, it reads higher today (9.6bp instead of 4.5bp for euro governments) because long government yields now sit above swaps. Everything on this page outside these two charts uses real swap curves.

    Why the fixed-rate figures are higher, and why that is not a rates story. A fixed-rate wedge has two parts: a credit part, close to the floating-rate wedge, and an interest-rate part from the slope of the swap curve between the average life and the final maturity. The EURIBOR swap curve rises only from 3.30% at 7 years to 3.38% at 13 years, so for euro governments the fixed-rate wedge of 13.8bp is 10.0bp of credit and 3.8bp of interest rate; across the segments the interest-rate part is 3.0–5.4bp. The rest of the gap between fixed and floating is a quoting effect: a YAS Z-spread compounds annually for euro bonds, and turning it into a half-yearly margin costs the floating-rate loan 0.6–8.1bp on euro curves (most for wide spreads such as Romania's). In the Portugal example the swap curve falls slightly beyond its 12.5-year average life (3.56% to 3.47% at 24 years), so the interest-rate part is −0.8bp and the two wedges are nearly the same (7.1 and 7.5bp).

    Against the last ten years, November 2026 is middling to low. For euro area governments the credit spread over the AAA curve gives a floating-rate wedge of 4.5bp, steeper than in only 34% of months since 2016; the all-in curve gives a fixed-rate wedge of 13.0bp, steeper than in 21%. For US high-grade companies the credit wedge, 7.3bp, is near the bottom of its range (5th percentile; median 18.1bp). Two things explain it:

    • Credit spreads are near their tightest in decades, and tight credit curves are flat curves. For US high-grade companies the two move together: across the ten years, months with wider spreads had a median wedge of 20.8bp, months with tight spreads 8.7bp. EMBI spreads for Mexico, Chile, Peru and Brazil sit around the 10th percentile of their range since 2016.
    • The 2026 hikes flattened the rates curves. The Fed raised rates in September and markets price a further hike in December; the ECB hiked in June and September. Short rates rose about a point in a year while long rates rose less: the 2s30s slope roughly halved on Treasuries and euro government curves, and on EURIBOR swaps it went from +82bp to −5bp. That took about a third off every fixed-rate wedge: euro area governments from 19.7 to 13.0bp a year, US high-grade companies from 18.6 to 14.4bp.

    But it is a far better moment than 2023–24 for fixed-rate loans. Then the rates curves were inverted at the short end and flat further out, and the all-in fixed-rate wedge was much smaller (2023 medians: 8.0bp for euro area governments, 9.8bp for US high-grade companies, against 13.0 and 14.4bp today).

    And the dispersion is wide. Measured over swaps, as YAS does, Romania, Indonesia (EUR), the Philippines, Mexico and Poland give 13.6–16.9bp a year on the same floating-rate loan, against 9.6bp for the euro government aggregate and 4.2–10.0bp for US investment-grade companies. A pitch today should pick borrowers rather than wait for the market.

    USD
    Good, but at risk

    The SOFR swap curve adds about 5.2bp to a 13-year fixed-rate loan. Markets price a Fed hike in December (CME FedWatch about 80%); in the "hikes and oil" scenario USD fixed-rate wedges fall 20–46%. For fixed-rate USD loans, sooner is better.

    Floating rate, everywhere
    Steady, with one tail risk

    A gradual widening of spreads steepens credit curves and lifts the wedge by a quarter to a third. A sudden shock does the opposite: short spreads jump most, curves flatten or invert, and most floating-rate wedges fall to zero or below. The IMF (GFSR, October 2026) names a disorderly rise in sovereign yields and an AI-led correction among its top risks.

    Fixed or floating? Let the borrower's needs decide, not the rates curve. Today the swap curves add 3.0–5.4bp a year to a fixed-rate wedge, about the size of the uncertainty in the swap data themselves (section 4). The credit curve does the work in both cases, and it is the credit curve that ranks the borrowers.

    7 · FINANCING NEEDS AHEAD

    Who will need long money in 2027

    The segments graded best are also borrowing heavily, from public reports of their budgets and issuance plans:

    • Central and eastern Europe: defence, grids and the coal exit, nuclear in Poland and Czechia, and EU co-financing. Romania planned about €10bn of euro bonds in 2026; Bulgaria came twice to fill its budget gap.
    • Morocco: 2030 World Cup infrastructure, high-speed rail, desalination, renewables and phosphates.
    • Latin America: copper and lithium in Chile and Peru; Mexico's state power and energy companies.
    • Asia: power, transport and energy-transition programmes in Indonesia and the Philippines, with oil adding to fiscal pressure.
    • Gulf: giga-projects at a time when lower oil revenue has widened deficits (Qatar's is near a decade high).
    • Everywhere: data centres. Hyperscalers are now about 10% of global investment-grade bond issuance (IMF).

    9 · DATA CHECKS

    Do yields, spreads and rates curves agree?

    Every input was checked before it went into the engine, in three layers. Against independent data: a new bond's yield should equal its benchmark (a Treasury, or the mid-swap rate) plus its spread on the pricing date; the swap curves should start where the official overnight and 6-month fixings are. Inside the engine: the Calculator's own flags, applied to every curve: the bridge coupon must reproduce the yield (Results rows 54–55) and the default intensity must not turn negative (row 52). Provenance: a curve resting on one press source, on guidance rather than final pricing, or on an assumed shape is marked.

    How the inputs are prepared.

    • Interpolation. Yields enter the engine at every year up to the last quoted maturity, with the Z-spread over swaps solved from each yield, so a fixed-rate loan sees the true shape of the swap curve between quoted maturities. Beyond the last quoted maturity the engine's own rule applies: the default intensity is held flat.
    • First fixing. The engine uses the official 6-month EURIBOR fixing (ECB, October average) and a 6-month SOFR rate for the first period.
    • Benchmarks. Dollar new issues are quoted over the nearest on-the-run Treasury; spreads are re-measured over a Treasury of the same maturity (Mexico's 12-year bond: T+200 over the 10-year, about +185bp over a 12-year Treasury).
    • Final pricing. Final pricing is used wherever it is published; where only guidance exists, the point is flagged (Chile's 20-year).
    • Re-levelling. Older Latin American deals are moved to today's level with the country's EMBI spread (Chile +12bp since July, Mexico +2bp since January).

    Open questions, not resolved here: the engine's closed-form bootstrap has no solution for some very high fixed coupons (for example a flat 9.6% half-yearly curve given only at 2 and 30 years), and the 1-year EURIBOR swap from the indicative source sits 15bp above what the fixing and the €STR curve imply.

    10 · HOW TO USE THIS

    A starting list, not a quote

    Each grade says where to look first. It does not say what a borrower will gain. The borrower's own bonds can sit above or below the segment curve, and a single new bond or a rating change can reshape it. Before a meeting:

    1. Take one Bloomberg YAS snapshot of the borrower's own bonds (Z-spread and YTM by maturity) in the loan's currency, and enter it in the Calculator.
    2. Try the loan's real profile and two or three tenors in the Simulator; replay the market episodes that worry the borrower.
    3. If the curve is flat or inverted, drop the wedge argument and lead with tenor and certainty of funding (playbook §9).
    4. Present with the one-pager and pitch; prepare with the Meeting Playbook.
    Method and data: how the numbers on this page were made

    Engine. Every wedge is computed by wal_wedge_engine.py, the Python mirror of the Calculator workbook: survival is bootstrapped from the borrower curve, the loan is priced mini-bond by mini-bond, and the result is compared with the curve read at the average life. Standard loan: 1-year drawdown, 24 half-yearly instalments, final maturity 13 years, average life 7.0 years. Long loan: 2-year drawdown, 36 instalments, final maturity 20 years, average life 10.5 years. Recovery 40%. EUR bonds pay annual coupons, USD bonds half-yearly.

    Rates curves. Today's figures use real swap curves: EURIBOR 6M for EUR loans (the Calculator's default basis, as in the Portugal example) and SOFR for USD loans, both 6 November 2026 closes from BlueGamma (indicative quotes). Spreads quoted over mid-swaps are used as they are; spreads quoted over Treasuries, and the euro government and corporate spreads measured over government curves, are re-based to swaps by adding the gap between the government curve and the swap curve at each maturity. The ten-year history uses government curves only (see section 3).

    Credit curves. Each segment is an all-in yield curve: a reference curve plus the borrower's spread over it. Measured: ECB all-government curve over the AAA curve (daily); ICE BofA US corporate option-adjusted spreads by maturity band and by rating (FRED); US Treasury HQM corporate curve (AA–A), converted from spot to par. New issue: the spreads and yields at which the borrower's 2026 bonds priced, re-measured over a reference of the same maturity and checked against the benchmark on the pricing date (section 9). Stylised: France and Italy use the shape of the ECB aggregate scaled to their 10-year spread; European companies and the high-yield ratings use the US investment-grade shape scaled to their measured level (single-B flat, CCC inverted). The Z-spread over swaps is then solved from each yield by inverting the engine's bridge, at every year up to the last quoted maturity; beyond it the engine holds the default intensity flat.

    History. Monthly averages from January 2016, built the same way as today's figures but with the government curve as the reference and discount curve, since no free swap history exists. The euro line ends on the latest daily reading (6 November). Percentiles compare today's reading with every month since then. The fixed-rate wedge is split as in the Calculator (Results rows 34–35): interest-rate part = forward rates weighted like the loan minus the risk-free par rate at the average life; credit part = the rest.

    Scenarios. Shifts applied to today's curves, then the engine is run again. Rate shifts move the swap curve and the government curve together, so the gap between them is kept; spread changes apply to the credit spread only. Base: US long rates 10bp lower, EUR 30-year 15bp higher (pension switch), credit spreads 10% wider. Hikes and oil: short rates +75bp, 10-year +25bp, credit +30bp short, +15bp long. Oil normalises: short rates −75 to −100bp, long rates −20bp or unchanged, credit 10% tighter. Gradual widening: credit 40% wider in proportion. Acute shock: credit +140bp at 2 years to +55bp at 30 years, government yields lower. Sizes follow published outlooks and the 2020 and 2022 episodes; they are illustrations, not forecasts.

    Every figure was computed on 9–10 November 2026 from the sources listed below; a refresh means downloading the series again and rerunning the engine.
