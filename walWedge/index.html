The WAL Wedge

01 · The question in plain words

Two ways to borrow EUR 5bn, one pricing habit

A borrower can raise EUR 5bn by issuing bonds or by taking an amortising loan. Lenders usually price the loan off the borrower's own bond curve, because that curve is the market's live view of the borrower's credit. The question is which point on that curve to use.

A bond repays everything at maturity. Our loan repays a little every six months for 23 years, so a lot of the money comes back early. The usual habit is to summarise the loan by its weighted average life (WAL), here 12.5 years, and read the bond curve at 12.5 years. We call this the shortcut.

The shortcut says

"This loan behaves like a 12.5-year bond, so charge the 12.5-year spread."

Z-spread at 12.5y = 53.5 bp · YTM at 12.5y = 4.0965%

The exact answer says

"Price every half-year of the loan at what that half-year actually costs on the bond curve, then average."

Fair spread = 62.3 bp · fair fixed rate = 4.1355%

The difference between the two is the wedge. When it is positive, the shortcut charges less than the borrower's own bond market would, so the borrower saves and the lender gives up margin relative to bond-equivalent pricing. When it is negative, the borrower overpays.

wedge = fair spread or rate − shortcut spread or rate (+ X)
fair
What this exact drawdown and repayment profile costs on the borrower's bond curve. Results row 13.
shortcut
The curve read at the WAL, plus any agreed margin X above or below it. Results rows 11–12.
Takeaway

The saving is a net figure. Before the WAL date the borrower overpays, because early exposure is genuinely cheaper than the 12.5-year point. After the WAL date it underpays, because late exposure is much dearer. On a rising curve the second effect wins. Sections 03 and 04 explain why it wins even when the curve is a straight line.

03 · Idea 1 · Quoted spreads are averages

A quoted spread is the average speed of the whole trip

Think of a car trip. If you averaged 42 km/h over ten hours of driving, that tells you nothing about how fast you were going in the tenth hour. You may have crawled through traffic early and driven fast at the end.

A bond curve works the same way. The 10-year Z-spread of 42bp is the average credit cost of years 1 to 10. Money that is lent in year 10 pays the cost of year 10 alone, which is called the forward cost. On a rising curve the forward cost is far above the average.

Peeling the curve, one year at a time

We can recover each year's own cost from the averages, like peeling an onion from the inside:

  • The 1-year bond covers only year 1, so year 1 costs −19bp.
  • The 2-year bond is the average of years 1 and 2. Year 1 is known, so year 2 follows.
  • Continue out the curve. Each new bond adds exactly one unknown year.

Peeled this way, with every year weighted equally, this is a simple-average approximation. The page's full pricing engine instead runs a full bootstrap: it solves, year by year, for the default intensity that reprices each bond exactly, with discounting and default risk included. The formula below is the simple-average version, useful for intuition; the table that follows compares it with the full-bootstrap numbers actually used elsewhere on this page.

cost of year b ≈ b × spread(b) − (b−1) × spread(b−1)
b × spread(b)
Total credit cost of years 1 to b, if each year counted equally.
(b−1) × spread(b−1)
Total cost of years 1 to b−1, already known.

This simple-average approximation treats every year as equally important. The full bootstrap also discounts later years and allows for default, so later years count less; a later year then needs a higher cost to move the average by the same amount. That is why the full-bootstrap figures below are a bit higher than the simple-average ones.

Trap: weighting the quoted curve flips the sign

It is tempting to skip the peeling step above and weight the quoted curve itself by how much of the loan is outstanding each year. Don't: a quoted point is already an average cost to that maturity, so every long-dated quote has the cheap early years baked into it. Weighting the quotes directly counts those early years again and again, which drags the result down toward the short end.

For this case that gives 18.0 bp — far below the 53.5bp actually charged — making it look as if the borrower overpays by 35.5bp. Peel the years first, as above, and the true fair cost is 62.3bp: the borrower is actually underpaying, by 8.82bp. Same inputs, opposite conclusion.

The fixed case shows the same reversal: weighting the quotes directly gives 3.610%, against the 4.0554% charged — an apparent 44.6bp overpayment — while the correct, peeled calculation gives +8.02bp: an underpayment.

Takeaway

Never price exposure in year t at the curve's quote for maturity t. Convert the curve into the cost of each year first. In this case year 10 alone costs about 99.0 bp, more than twice the 42bp the 10-year quote shows.

04 · Idea 2 · Same WAL is not same timing

WAL measures how much. It does not measure when.

WAL counts principal-years: how much money is lent, for how long. It says nothing about where in time those principal-years sit. Two loans with the same WAL can put their money in very different years, and on a rising curve different years cost different amounts.

Rectangles and triangles

Draw the balance outstanding against time. The area is the WAL. The balance point of the shape, its centre of gravity, is the average date at which money is lent out.

  • Bullet maturing at W: the full amount is out from 0 to W. The shape is a rectangle; it balances in the middle, at W/2.
  • Straight amortiser with the same WAL: the balance falls in a straight line to zero. The shape is a triangle. To have the same area it must be twice as wide (maturity 2W), and a triangle balances one-third of the way from its tall side, at 2W/3.

For our loan the centre of gravity is 8.26 years, against 6.25 years for a 12.5-year bullet (ratio 0.661, close to the 2/3 of a pure straight amortiser; the drawdown year and the half-yearly steps shift it slightly).

WAL is also a centre of gravity, of a different thing

WAL

Centre of gravity of the repayments. Answers: on average, when is the money paid back?

Exposure centre

Centre of gravity of the balance outstanding. Answers: on average, when is the money lent out? This is what the credit curve prices.

The exposure centre: a baseline plus a dispersion term

exposure centre = WAL / 2 + (spread of repayment dates)² ÷ (2 × WAL)
WAL / 2
Where a bullet's exposure sits.
spread²
The variance of the repayment dates. Zero for a bullet (one date); large for a loan repaid over 23 years.

The wedge ultimately comes from how dispersed the loan's repayments are over time — a dimension WAL does not capture at all, since WAL only tracks their average date. This exposure centre has no standard name in finance; "exposure-weighted average time" is a reasonable description. Its closest relatives are WAL, the centre of gravity of the repayments themselves, and Macaulay duration, the textbook centre of gravity of discounted cash flows (the classic seesaw picture). Section 05 defines both properly, with diagrams, geometry and units, and sets them beside Macaulay duration.

Where this formula comes from, and what it proves

This drops out of one of the oldest identities in statistics: for any set of weighted points, the second moment — the average of their squared distance from zero — equals the variance plus the mean squared. Apply that to the repayment dates, weighted by how much principal each one repays (exactly as WAL already weights them), and the exposure centre — which is half that second moment divided by WAL — falls out directly as WAL/2 plus variance ÷ (2 × WAL). No assumption about the shape of the repayment schedule is needed; it holds for any profile.

It checks out exactly against both shapes derived from geometry above. A bullet has a single repayment date, so its variance is zero and the formula collapses to WAL/2 — the rectangle's midpoint. A pure straight-line amortiser repays uniformly between zero and its maturity M; a uniform distribution's variance is M²/12, and feeding that in gives exactly M/3 — the triangle's centroid, and 2 × WAL/3 once WAL = M/2 is substituted in. The formula does not just match these two special cases, it explains why every profile in between behaves the way it does.

Because variance can never be negative, the formula also proves something stronger than either worked example alone: at a given WAL, the exposure centre can never sit earlier than WAL/2, and a bullet is the only shape that reaches that floor. Spreading repayments out over time — which is exactly what amortisation does — always adds a non-negative penalty on top. The more dispersed the schedule, the larger that penalty, and the further the exposure centre sits beyond WAL/2. That is the exact mechanism behind this loan's 8.26-year exposure centre landing well past the 6.25 years an equal-WAL bullet would show: 23 years of semi-annual repayments is a far more dispersed schedule than a single bullet date, and the formula prices that dispersion directly into extra years.

Why a straight curve still produces a wedge

It is natural to assume the wedge needs a curved curve. It does not. A perfectly straight curve produces one too, and the exposure centre explains why. Two facts combine.

1 · On a straight line, a loan prices at a single point

Averaging a straight line gives the same answer as reading it once, at the average. So a loan's fair cost is simply the cost at its exposure centre: the bullet at WAL/2, the amortiser at 2 × WAL/3. On a rising line the amortiser's later centre means a higher cost — with no curvature anywhere.

2 · The quoted curve rises at half the true rate

A quoted point is an average of every year up to it, and an average always lags what it averages. If the quoted curve rises by g per year, each individual year's cost rises by 2g. The shortcut reads the shallow curve; the loan actually pays the steep one.

Straight curve starting at 10bpYear 1Year 2Year 3Year 4
Quoted curve — rises 4bp a year10 bp14 bp18 bp22 bp
Cost of that year alone, simple-average peel — rises 8bp a year10 bp18 bp26 bp34 bp

Put the two facts together and, for a straight curve, the wedge collapses to a rule of thumb:

wedge ≈ quoted-curve slope g × WAL ÷ 3
g
How fast the quoted curve rises, in bp per year.
÷ 3
Carries the dispersion of a straight amortiser's repayments. A bullet has no dispersion, so its wedge is exactly zero: the shortcut prices it correctly.

For a curve of 10bp rising 4bp a year and our loan: 4 × 12.5 ÷ 3 = 16.7 bp, against the engine's exact 13.55 bp. The rule of thumb counts every year equally; the engine discounts later years and allows for default, which gives the long tail less weight and brings the figure down.

How the two facts combine into the rule of thumb

Write the straight curve. Let the quoted curve be quoted(t) = a + g × t: it starts at a and rises g each year. Fact 2 says the per-year costs it averages must rise twice as fast, so cost(t) = a + 2g × t. (Check it: average a + 2g × t from 0 to T and the quoted curve comes back out as a + g × T.)

What the borrower is charged. The shortcut reads the quoted curve at the WAL: charged = a + g × WAL.

What is actually fair. Fact 1 says the loan prices at a single point — its exposure centre E — and pays the per-year cost there: fair = a + 2g × E.

Subtract the two. wedge = (a + 2g × E) − (a + g × WAL) = g × (2E − WAL). The level a cancels out, which is exactly why the rule of thumb depends on the slope alone and not on where the curve sits.

That leaves one number to find per shape, and both were derived earlier:

ShapeExposure centre E2E − WALWedge
BulletWAL / 200
Straight amortiser2 × WAL / 3WAL / 3g × WAL / 3

So the ÷ 3 is simply 2 × (2/3) − 1: what is left after doubling the amortiser's exposure centre and taking off the WAL the shortcut charges at. A bullet sits exactly at WAL/2, so the two prices meet and its wedge is zero. Every amortiser sits beyond WAL/2, so every amortiser has a positive wedge.

From there the shape of the curve decides the size:

  • Flat curve: every year costs the same, so timing cannot matter. Wedge ≈ 0. In this case the engine returns 0.002 bp: a technical residue because the model holds default intensity constant within each year while the first six months use the 6M fixing. Without the 6M fixing it is 0.000 bp.
  • Straight and rising: wedge proportional to the slope.
  • Curved: a curve that flattens at the long end (the usual shape) shrinks the wedge, because the loan's tail lands where costs grow more slowly. A curve that steepens further out enlarges it.
Takeaway

Equal WAL means equal quantity of lending, not equal timing. An amortiser always has its exposure later than the equal-WAL bullet. On any rising curve that alone makes it dearer. The early-year loss and the late-year gain do not cancel, even on a straight line.

05 · Deep dive · The two measures of timing

WAL and the exposure centre, defined properly

Two numbers describe when a facility's money is at risk: the WAL and the exposure centre. Both are averages of dates measured in years, both have appeared already, and they are constantly mistaken for one another. Everything from here on rests on telling them apart, so this section defines each one properly, shows what it looks like, and sets them side by side.

WAL, defined two ways

WAL answers one question: how much lending is there, and for how long? There are two routes to it. They look like different quantities — one watches the loan's balance, the other follows individual euros — and they always land on the same number. Both are set out below, then the reason they agree.

Way 1 · by date — watch the balance

Stand at each date in turn and ask how much is still owed at that moment. Multiply by how long that moment lasts, and add up across the life of the loan. In words: how much is out, and for how long, totted up period by period. Geometrically it is the area under the balance-outstanding curve.

WAL = Σ Oi · a
Oi
The balance outstanding in half-year i, as a fraction of the original principal. It starts at 1 and steps down to 0.
a
The length of each period, 0.5 years. Half-years are used because the loan pays semi-annually.

Units: Oi is a fraction, so fraction × years = years. Note that a division is already hidden inside Oi. Had the balance been written in euros, the sum would come out in euro-years, and you would still have to divide by the principal to get back to years — which is exactly what expressing the balance as a fraction does up front.

Way 2 · by euro — follow the money

Forget the balance and take the euros one at a time. Each euro is drawn on some date and repaid on some later date, and the gap between the two is how long that euro was working. Average that gap over every euro borrowed. In words: the average time a euro stays out.

WAL = average repayment date − average drawdown date = 12.75 − 0.25 = 12.50
average repayment date
Each repayment date weighted by how much principal comes back on it. Here the 46 instalments are equal and evenly spaced, so their weighted average is simply the midpoint of the first and the last: (1.5 + 24) ÷ 2 = 12.75 years.
average drawdown date
The same calculation on the way in. Here half the money is drawn at signing and half at month 6: (½ × 0) + (½ × 0.5) = 0.25 years. Subtracting it is what stops the loan being credited for time before the money was actually advanced.

Units: years − years = years. Both terms are averages weighted by principal, so each has already been divided by the amount borrowed. When everything is drawn at once on day one the second term is zero and WAL is simply the average repayment date — which is why a bullet's WAL equals its maturity.

Why the two agree

Put side by side, the two ways share one thing: both are totalling euro-years — one euro being outstanding for one year. Way 1 collects euro-years by date, Way 2 collects the same euro-years by euro. Neither can be larger than the other, because they are inventories of the same thing.

That also settles the units. The raw total is genuinely in euro-years: this loan consumes 5bn × 12.5 = 62.5bn euro-years. WAL reports that total per euro borrowed, so the euros cancel and what is left is years — read it as "the average euro stayed out for 12.5 years". Both formulas above have that division already built in, which is why neither shows it explicitly.

To see it, picture the loan as a physical stack of money. Stack the principal up with the slice that will be repaid first on top. As each instalment is paid the stack is shaved off the top, so the outline of the stack over time is exactly the balance-outstanding curve. Shade the whole stack over its life: every square of that shape is one euro-year, and the total area is every euro-year the loan consumes. Sweeping that area vertically is Way 1; sweeping it horizontally is Way 2.

That is the whole reason "average time a euro stays out" and "total principal-years per euro" are one number. The picture also shows, at a glance, why WAL is blind to dispersion: an area can be pushed around in time, stretched late or bunched early, without its total changing at all. Two loans with very different shapes can cover identical area — which is exactly the bullet-versus-amortiser case in section 04.

The exposure centre: where the shape balances

The WAL is the area of that stack of money. The exposure centre asks a different question of the same shape: not how big it is, but where it sits. Cut the shape out of card and rest it on a knife edge. The point where it balances is the exposure centre.

This is worth doing for the two shapes section 04 drew. Both carry the same WAL of 12.5 years — the same area, the same euro-years — so by the WAL they are the same loan. They do not balance in the same place.

The definition · as a sum

Go through the half-years one at a time, note the date, and weight it by how much is still outstanding then. Periods carrying a lot of balance pull the average towards themselves; periods carrying none have no say at all.

The difference from WAL is smaller than it looks. Way 1 added up the euro-years in each half-year, Σ Oi·a, and stopped there — a total, with no mention of when any of it happened. The exposure centre takes those same euro-years and tags each one with its date before averaging. That extra factor, the date, is the whole difference between the two measures.

exposure centre = Σ ti·Oi·a ÷ WAL
ti
The middle of half-year i, in years from signing. The middle rather than the end, because the balance sits there for the whole period, not just at its close.
Oi·a
The euro-years consumed in that half-year: the balance outstanding, as a fraction of principal, times the 0.5-year length of the period. Exactly one column of the stack from Way 1.
WAL
The total euro-years, since that is all the WAL is. Dividing by it is what turns a total into an average.

So the whole thing reads: every euro-year, tagged with the date it happened, divided by all the euro-years. Units: (years × years) ÷ years = years. The euro-years cancel, and with them every trace of how much exposure there is. What survives is purely when it sits — which is why this, and not the WAL, is the number the credit curve prices.

The same number · as a split

That sum can be rearranged into two pieces that each mean something on their own: a baseline every loan starts from, and a penalty for spreading repayments out in time. Section 04 derives it and uses it to explain the wedge; it is repeated here because it is the clearest statement of what the exposure centre actually measures.

exposure centre = WAL / 2 + (spread of repayment dates)² ÷ (2 × WAL)
WAL / 2
The baseline: where a loan balances if all its exposure is concentrated on a single date. No loan can sit earlier than this.
spread²
The variance of the repayment dates: spread is their standard deviation, how far they typically sit from the WAL, so squaring it gives years squared. Zero for a bullet, which has one date; large for 46 instalments running over 23 years. Written the same way in section 04.

Units: years + (years² ÷ years) = years. That is what the division by 2 × WAL is for — without it the two terms could not be added. Because a variance can never be negative, the second term only ever pushes the centre later.

Where the split comes from

The split is not a second formula. It is the sum above, rewritten in four steps — and the steps are worth following, because they are also where the lone 2 enters.

  1. Take one euro. A euro drawn at the start and repaid after t years is outstanding on every day in between — one horizontal row of the stack in the first diagram. It therefore contributes t euro-years, and since it is out evenly across that whole span, those euro-years are centred on the middle of the row, at t/2. That halving is the only 2 in the formula.
  2. Weigh the euros against each other. Each euro has its own little balance point at t/2, and the balance point of the whole shape is the average of them. Not a plain average, though: a euro that stayed out for 20 years puts 20 euro-years of weight on the shape, while one that stayed out for 2 years puts only 2. So each euro's centre is weighted by its own euro-years — its t — giving Σ t × (t/2) ÷ Σ t.
  3. Turn the sums into averages. Divide the top and the bottom of that fraction by the number of euros. Nothing changes, but both sums become averages. The bottom becomes the average time a euro stays out, which is the WAL from Way 2. The ½ on top is the same for every euro, so it lifts out of the average. What remains is average(t²) ÷ (2 × WAL).
  4. Split the average of t². One of the standard identities of statistics says the average of the squares equals the variance plus the square of the average: average(t²) = spread² + WAL². Substituting gives (spread² + WAL²) ÷ (2 × WAL), and separating the two terms leaves WAL² ÷ (2 × WAL), which is just WAL/2, plus spread² ÷ (2 × WAL).

So the 2 is never applied twice. There is one division by 2 × WAL, written first as a single fraction and then shared out between the two terms. Steps 1 and 2 together also explain why dispersion can only push the centre later: a euro that stays out twice as long sits twice as late and carries twice the weight, so its pull on the average grows with the square of its life. Stretching a schedule out therefore moves the centre out more than shortening it moves the centre in.

Closed forms for the two basic shapes

For most schedules the sum above is the only way to get the answer. But for the two shapes in the diagram the answer is known in advance, straight from geometry — a formula that hands you the result rather than making you add up sixty terms. That is what a closed form is.

bullet = WAL / 2      straight amortiser = 2 × WAL / 3
bullet
A rectangle balances in the middle. The bullet's exposure is spread evenly from day one to maturity, so it balances at half its length. It is also the split above with a variance of zero: a bullet has a single repayment date, so there is no dispersion to add.
straight amortiser
A triangle balances one third of the way along from its tall side. To hold the same area, the amortiser's triangle has to run twice as long as the bullet, and one third of that double length lands at 2 × WAL / 3.

With WAL = 12.5 years: the bullet balances at 6.25, the amortiser at 8.33. Both figures are marked on the diagram above.

So the three boxes above are not three different quantities. The sum is the definition, the split is that same sum rearranged to show what drives it, and the closed forms are shortcuts that exist only when the shape is simple enough to recognise. A real schedule — a drawdown ramp, uneven instalments, a grace period — is not a clean triangle, so the model ignores the shortcuts and computes the weighted average directly. That is why this loan lands at 8.26 years rather than exactly on the 8.33 the triangle predicts.

06 · Credit, default and recovery

Two questions: how likely is default, and how much is lost

Ideas 1 and 2 were about when a loan is exposed. This section is about what that exposure costs. A lender's margin has to cover its expected loss, and an expected loss is always the same two things multiplied together: the chance the borrower defaults, and the share of the money that does not come back. Everything below is one of those two.

credit margin ≈ λ × (1 − R)   ⇔   λ ≈ credit margin ÷ (1 − R)
λ
How likely. The default intensity, or hazard rate: the chance of defaulting in a given year.
1 − R
How much is lost. The share not recovered: 60% with 40% recovery.
margin
Floating: the Z-spread. Fixed: the YTM minus the swap rate of the same maturity (both in semi-annual terms).

Year 10: the year's own cost is 99.0 bp, so λ ≈ 99 ÷ 0.6 ≈ 165bp; the exact model gives 159.6 bp. Dividing by 0.6 slightly overshoots: what is lost on default is the principal plus the half-year's interest, less recovery, so the exact break-even is c ≈ λ × (1 − R + f·a) ≈ λ × 0.617 here (section 10).

How likely: the default intensity λ

The model expresses each year's credit cost as a default intensity λ, also called a hazard rate.

λyear b = probability of defaulting during year b, given no default so far

λ = 1.6% in year 10 means: if the borrower reaches year 10 alive, there is about a 1.6% chance it defaults during year 10. The chances chain into a survival probability, S(t) = exp(−sum of λ up to t), the chance of still being alive at t.

The model does not use the shortcut in the box above. For every year b, it finds the λ that makes a bond maturing at b worth exactly 100, valuing it half-year by half-year: coupons if the borrower survives, 40% if it defaults, all discounted on the swap curve. Earlier years are already solved, so each new year has one unknown, and the equation is a quadratic with a closed-form answer. No Goal Seek, no iteration.

Why the workbook shows "Negative forward hazard: YES"

This issuer's 1–3 year bonds trade below swaps: Z-spreads of −19, −15 and −14bp, and YTMs below the swap rates (3.032% against 3.270% at 1 year). The only default intensity that prices a bond richer than the risk-free curve is a negative one, so λ comes out around −31, −18 and −19bp for years 1–3. A real probability cannot be negative, hence the flag.

The calculation stays valid: every bond reprices to its quote and the integrity checks read OK. It simply means the market treats this issuer as safer than the swap curve at the short end, which is common for very high-grade names whose short bonds are scarce or in demand. It also explains why S(1) = 1.0031, slightly above 1. Before relying on the result, confirm the Z-spreads were measured against the same swap curve you entered, on the same date.

These are market-implied, not real-world, probabilities

λ is backed out of prices. Bond spreads also pay for uncertainty, liquidity and supply and demand, so implied default probabilities are usually well above historical default rates for the same rating. For this calculator that is exactly right: the goal is to price the loan consistently with where the borrower's bonds trade, whatever is driving those prices. λ is a pricing tool, not a default forecast.

How much is lost: recovery, and the balance it applies to

Standard practice assumes a lender recovers 40% of what it is owed when a senior unsecured borrower defaults. That fixes the rate. The question that decides the answer is what the rate is applied to.

loss in half-year i = (Si−1 − Si) × (1 + f·a − R) × Oi
Si−1 − Si
The chance of defaulting in that half-year, having survived to its start. This is the λ half, above.
1 + f·a − R
Lost per unit still owed: the principal, plus the half-year's interest due, less what is recovered.
Oi
The balance still outstanding in that half-year. Severity applies to what is owed then, not to the original EUR 5bn.

The same Oi appears in the recovery term of the loan's present value, R·(Si−1 − Si)·Oi, and in the weights wi that average the per-period costs (section 10).

For a bullet bond the distinction is invisible: the balance is 100% of face for the whole life, so "40% of what is owed" and "40% of the face" are the same number. You cannot get it wrong. For an amortising loan the two part company every six months, and only one of them is right.

Suppose the borrower defaults in year 20. About 19.57% of the original EUR 5bn is still outstanding then, so the lender recovers 40% of that: roughly EUR 0.39bn. Applying 40% to the original EUR 5bn instead would claim EUR 2bn of recovery on a position where under EUR 1bn is still owed. The loss given default would turn negative — the model would treat a late default as a windfall — and the late years, which is where the wedge lives, would price as almost free.

Why the recovery assumption itself barely matters

Changing the recovery rate moves the answer very little: 8.99 bp at 20% recovery, 8.82 at 40%, 8.49 at 60% (section 08). That looks surprising until you notice that R enters the model twice, in opposite directions.

Going in: backing out λ

The bond quotes are fixed market data. A higher recovery means each default costs less, so a higher λ is needed to explain the same observed spread.

Coming out: pricing the loan

That higher λ is then multiplied by a smaller loss per default. The two moves are close to proportional, so most of the change cancels.

Takeaway

The recovery rate is a soft assumption: the model is calibrated to the borrower's own bonds, and it largely absorbs whatever rate you choose. The recovery base is not. Getting the base wrong collapses the cost of the late years, and the late years are exactly what Ideas 1 and 2 showed the wedge is made of. This section is what keeps those two ideas from being quietly undone by an accounting error.

How the swap curve enters

Floating: a background role

The credit information is entirely in the Z-spread. The swap curve supplies the discount factors and the floating coupons. Raising the whole swap curve by 30bp moves the saving from 8.82 to 8.70 bp.

The one hard requirement: the Z-spreads must be measured against the same swap curve you enter.

Fixed: it splits rate from credit

The swap curve decides how much of each YTM is risk-free rate and how much is credit. A 30bp higher swap curve leaves about 30bp less credit at every tenor, lowering every λ by roughly 50bp, while the risk-free forwards rise by about the same amount. The total, anchored by the YTM curve, moves only from 8.02 to 8.89 bp. What moves is the split shown in Results rows 34–35.

Milestone

You now have a cost for every half-year of the next 30 years, built from the borrower's own bonds. Next: apply those costs to the loan.

09 · Try it

The lab

The full engine runs in this page. It starts on the case above; change anything and every number updates. Curve scenarios transform the case's own curves so you can see what drives the answer.

Check yourself

Set the curve to "Flat". What happens to the floating saving, and why?

It drops to about zero. When every year costs the same, timing stops mattering, so the WAL shortcut is exact. The tiny residue (0.002bp) comes from the model holding λ constant within each year while the first half-year uses the 6M fixing.

Set drawdown to 0 and repayment to 0.5 years. Why is the saving almost zero?

That loan is a six-month bullet: one repayment date, no dispersion of repayments, so its exposure centre is WAL/2, exactly like a bond. The saving is 0.03bp, not zero, for a technical reason: the shortcut reads the 1-year quote (held flat below 1 year), while the model prices the first six months using the 6M fixing, which differs slightly from the second half of year 1.

Try "Inverted". Who benefits now?

The lender. Late years are now cheaper than the WAL point, so the shortcut overcharges and the saving turns negative. The workbook reports it as negative and never floors it at zero.

Keep 23 years of repayment and lengthen the drawdown period to 3 and then 5 years. What happens?

The floating saving roughly doubles and then triples: 8.82bp with 1 year of drawdown, 17.32bp with 3 years, 25.23bp with 5 years (PV €39.7m, €79.3m, €116.5m on EUR 5bn). The shortcut point moves only from 12.5 to 14.5 years, but the loan's tail moves into years 24–28, where this curve implies its highest yearly costs (λ of about 290–325bp in years 23–25). A small move in WAL hides a large move in where the risk sits.

10 · For specialists

The full maths, annotated

Notation: half-year periods i = 1…60, ti = i/2, accrual a = 0.5, recovery R. All quantities per unit of notional.

Risk-free discount factors

DF(k) = (1 − rk·α·Σj<kDF(j)) ÷ (1 + rk·α)    DF(k−½) = √(DF(k−1)·DF(k))    DF(½) = 1 ÷ (1 + r6M·182.5/360)
rk
Par swap rate for year k (annual fixed leg). α = 1 for EUR vs 6M EURIBOR (30/360), 365.25/360 for €STR or USD SOFR (ACT/360).
r6M
6M EURIBOR fixing, 3.0190% here, giving DF(0.5) = 0.984926. Without it the half-year point is interpolated log-linearly.
fi·a
= DF(ti−1)/DF(ti) − 1: the simple forward rate for the half-year, so a riskless floater prices exactly at par.

Bootstrapping λ in closed form

Within year b, λb is constant. Let u = e−λb/2, the half-year survival ratio. A par bullet maturing at b, with years 1…b−1 already solved, satisfies:

1 = K + D1·Sp·[u·g1 + R(1−u)] + D2·Sp·[u²·g2 + R(u − u²)]
u = (−a1 + √(a1² − 4a2a0)) ÷ 2a2,   a2 = D2Sp(g2−R),   a1 = Sp(D1(g1−R) + D2R),   a0 = K + D1SpR − 1,   λb = −2 ln u
K
Value of the bond's cash flows in years 1…b−1: Σ DF[S·(coupon) + R·ΔS]. Floating coupon f·a + q·a, fixed coupon q·a (q = Z-spread or semi-annual YTM).
Sp
Survival at the start of year b. D1, D2: discount factors at b−½ and b. g1, g2: coupon (plus principal on g2) paid at those dates.
check
Every quoted bond reprices to its input; the workbook shows the maximum error (about 10−12 bp). Beyond the last quote, the last λ is extended, not the last spread.

Loan profile

Oi = min(i, D)/D − max(0, min(i−1, n) − D)/(2y),   D = 2x drawdowns, n = 2x + 2y,   WAL = Σ Oi·a
drawn
Drawdowns of 1/D at t = 0, 0.5, …, x − 0.5 (one drawdown of 1 at t = 0 if x = 0).
repaid
Instalments of 1/(2y) at t = x + 0.5, …, x + y.

Per-period break-even cost (exact decomposition)

ci = (1 − pi)·(1 + fia − R) ÷ (pi·a),   pi = Si/Si−1
1 − p
Chance of defaulting in the half-year, given survival to its start.
1 + fa − R
What is lost if default happens: principal plus the forward interest due, less recovery. Hence c ≈ λ(1 − R + fa), slightly above λ(1 − R).
ki
Floating: ki = ci. Fixed: ki = fi + ci.

Fair price and saving

wi = DFi·Si·a·Oi    fair = Σ wiki ÷ Σ wi    PV saving = N · Σ wi(ki − charged)
fair
Equivalent to solving PV(loan) = PV(drawdowns) for the flat spread or rate, where PV(loan) = Σ DFi[Si(couponiOi + Pi) + R(Si−1 − Si)Oi] and drawdowns are valued at DF·S on their dates. Proved by telescoping the per-period par conditions; the workbook checks it to about 10−7 euros.
split
Summing over periods before and after the WAL date (the straddling period split pro rata in time) gives the loss and benefit phases, which add up exactly to the net.
shortcut
Linear interpolation of the quoted curve at the exact WAL; for fixed, converted to semi-annual: 2((1 + y/m)m/2 − 1), m = 1 for EUR bonds.
11 · Questions people ask

Questions and answers

What if the bond curve is flat? What if it is straight?

Flat: every year costs the same, so it does not matter when the money is lent and the shortcut is right (saving ≈ 0; 0.002 bp in this case, a modelling residue). Straight and rising: there is a saving roughly equal to slope × WAL ÷ 3, because the amortiser's exposure sits later than the bullet's. Curvature then adds (steepening) or subtracts (flattening). See section 04.

Isn't the WAL already a centre of gravity? Why is it different?

WAL is the centre of gravity of the repayments. The credit curve prices the balance outstanding, whose centre of gravity is WAL/2 for a bullet and about 2·WAL/3 for a straight amortiser (8.26 years for this loan vs 6.25 for the bullet). There is no standard name; "exposure-weighted average time" describes it. Macaulay duration is the textbook relative.

Does the model recompute a centre of gravity for every half-year?

No. The centre of gravity is a teaching device and is exact only for straight curves. The model never computes it. It takes each half-year's balance (from the schedule, computed once), each half-year's cost (from the curve) and each half-year's weight, and averages. That works for any curve shape.

Where do the "share of weighting" percentages come from?

Each half-year's weight is balance × 0.5 × discount factor × survival. A block's share is its weights divided by the total, 9.002. The 18–24 year block holds 6.8% of the principal-years but only 3.5% of the weight, because those euros are far in the future and only collected if the borrower survives. The share is not multiplied by the balance again, and the block's cost (153.5 bp) comes from the curve, not from the loan.

Which "spread" drives λ: the Z-spread or the whole YTM?

Floating: the Z-spread directly, since it is already a spread over swaps. Fixed: the YTM minus the swap rate of the same maturity. The model does not subtract explicitly; it values fixed bonds on the swap curve and attributes whatever yield the swap curve cannot explain to λ. At 1 year: YTM 3.032% annual = 3.009% semi-annual, minus swap 3.270%, gives about −26bp of credit.

Is λ a real probability of default?

No, it is market-implied. It includes everything investors charge for, not only expected losses, so it is usually well above historical default rates, and here it is even negative at the short end. It is the right input for pricing consistently with the borrower's bonds, and the wrong input for a credit risk forecast.

Why does the workbook warn "Negative forward hazard"?

Because the short bonds trade below swaps. A bond can only be richer than the risk-free curve if the implied default intensity is negative. The pricing remains internally consistent; the flag asks you to confirm the data (same swap curve as the Z-spreads, same date).

Why is row 12 different from row 11 when the margin X is zero?

Floating: it isn't; 53.5 bp and 0.5350% are the same number. Fixed: the annual-coupon bond yield (4.0965%) is restated as the half-yearly rate with the same effective yield (4.0554%), so it can be compared with the fair rate, which is computed on the loan's half-yearly payments.

What is the risky annuity in row 9?

The sum of all half-year weights: the loan's principal-years after discounting and default risk. It converts basis points into today's money: 1bp a year on this loan is worth 1bp × 9.002 × EUR 5bn ≈ €4.5m. In CDS markets the same quantity is called the risky PV01 (RPV01).

What is the 6M fixing for?

The swap curve starts at 1 year, but the model needs a discount factor every six months. The 6M EURIBOR fixing tells it the actual rate for the first six months instead of assuming both halves of year 1 are equal. Its effect on the saving is far below 0.1bp; it is optional.

How much does the swap curve matter? Can we use free data?

Very little for the result: a 30bp error in the whole curve moves the floating saving from 8.82 to 8.70 bp and the fixed from 8.02 to 8.89 bp. Free sources with a short delay are adequate. The bond curves are what drive the answer.

Why are the loss and the benefit not equal on a straight curve?

Because the loan's exposure centre is later than the equal-WAL bullet's. The benefit side reaches further up the curve than the loss side reaches down it. See the rectangle-and-triangle figure in section 04.

Why is the fixed "risk-free effect" negative here?

The swap curve rises to 3.51% at 15 years and then falls to 3.29% at 30. Risk-free forwards in the loan's late years are therefore below the 12.5-year par rate. Reading the fixed rate at 12.5 years slightly overstates the risk-free part for this profile (−1.23 bp), while the credit part is understated by 9.25 bp. Net, the borrower still saves 8.02 bp.

Does the lender lose money?

Not in an accounting sense. The saving is margin the lender forgoes relative to pricing the loan consistently with the borrower's bond curve. Whether that is acceptable depends on relationship, ancillary business, funding and capital, none of which this calculator models. Use it to know the price of the convention, then decide.

What does recovery change?

Almost nothing for the result: from 8.99 bp (20%) to 8.49 bp (60%) on the floating side. A higher recovery raises the implied λ and lowers the loss per default by the same proportion. What matters is applying recovery to the balance outstanding, not to the original amount.

12 · Read before quoting a number

Assumptions and limitations

  • Bond quotes as par bonds. Each Z-spread is treated as the spread of a floating bullet at par for that tenor, each YTM as the coupon of a par bullet. Exact for bonds near par; for deep discount or premium lines, prefer a fitted issuer curve (Bloomberg CRVF / BVAL).
  • Loan mechanics. Semi-annual periods, accrual 0.5; floating resets to the 6M forward. Interest on drawn balances only; no commitment fee unless entered as a fee. Drawdowns are equal half-yearly amounts from signing.
  • Fixed rate basis. The charged fixed rate is the semi-annual equivalent of YTM(WAL) + X. If the term sheet uses the annual number as a half-yearly coupon, add the difference through X.
  • Single curve. The same swap curve projects and discounts. No basis between loan and bond funding, no bond new-issue premium unless entered (Inputs rows 22–24).
  • Beyond the data. Inside the quotes, years are interpolated linearly; beyond the last bond quote the last λ is extended and flagged. Maximum life 30 years.
  • Validation. The engine reproduces the reference specification exactly (65.8781bp vs 55.0000bp, wedge +10.8781bp) and, for this case, matches the workbook to the cent. Integrity checks in the workbook: bond repricing error, PV identity, WAL cross-check.
