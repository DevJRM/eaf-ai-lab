01 · The question in plain words

Two ways to borrow EUR 1bn, one pricing habit

In this sectionThe question and the two answers: the shortcut against the exact price. Next on the route: Stage 1 · The market data.

A borrower can raise EUR 1bn by issuing bonds or by taking an amortising loan. Lenders usually price the loan off the borrower's own bond curve, because that curve is the market's live view of the borrower's credit. The question is which point on that curve to use.

A bond repays everything at maturity. Our loan repays a little every six months for 23 years, so a lot of the money comes back early. The usual habit is to summarise the loan by its weighted average life (WAL), here 12.5 years, and read the bond curve at 12.5 years. We call this the shortcut.

The shortcut says

"This loan behaves like a 12.5-year bond, so charge the 12.5-year spread."

Z-spread at 12.5y = 53.5 bp · YTM at 12.5y = 4.0965% annual = 4.0554% half-yearly

The exact answer says

"Price every half-year of the loan at what that half-year actually costs on the bond curve, then average."

Fair spread = 60.6 bp · fair fixed rate = 4.1305% half-yearly

Both fixed rates above are quoted on the loan's own half-yearly basis, so they can be compared directly: 4.1305 − 4.0554 = 7.5 bp. EUR bonds quote an annual yield, and 4.0965% annual is the same yield as 4.0554% paid half-yearly; section 02 (stage 2) shows the conversion and why it matters.

!

The difference between the two is the wedge. When it is positive, the shortcut charges less than the borrower's own bond market would, so the borrower saves and the lender gives up margin relative to bond-equivalent pricing. When it is negative, the borrower overpays.

Z-spread: Bloomberg's YAS measure, and the par floating spread the model needs

In simple terms. Bloomberg's YAS Z-spread answers one question: by how much must every swap discount rate be raised so that the real bond's payments are worth its market price? It is one extra discount rate, with no view on default: the spread itself carries all the credit risk. On this case it is −19bp at 1 year, 42bp at 10 years and 92bp at 28. Because it is measured against the swap curve, the risk-free part has already been taken out.

A floating loan's margin answers a different question: what margin over EURIBOR would a six-monthly floating bond of the same issuer have to pay to be worth exactly 100, given that it pays only while the issuer survives and recovers 40% if it defaults? That is the par floating spread 8a · Par-float, and it is the unit of every floating price on this page. Same credit, different units: at 20 years the YAS Z-spread is 78bp and the par floating spread 76.0bp; at 10 years 42bp and 41.0bp.

Why they differ. The YAS spread is compounded once a year, like the bond's coupon, while the margin is paid every six months. The YAS spread applies to every euro the bond pays, the final repayment included, while a margin is paid only on coupon dates and only while the issuer survives. And on default the floating bond still recovers 40%. Together these are worth about 2bp at 20 years, and less at shorter maturities.

From one to the other: the bridge 12b · Bridge-coupon. You cannot get from 78bp to 76.0bp by a fixed adjustment (the gap is 2bp at 20 years but 1bp at 10), because the two numbers measure the credit in different ways. The Z-spread is one extra discount rate: it says how much to mark down the bond's payments, but nothing about how likely the issuer is to default or how much would be recovered. The par floating spread is calculated from exactly those two things. But both numbers describe the same thing: what the issuer's 20-year bond is worth. So the model uses that bond as a stepping stone, in three steps. First it rebuilds the bond the Z-spread describes: the coupon a bond priced at 100 must pay. Then it finds the default risk that explains that bond's price. Finally it prices a floating bond with that default risk. Section 11 works through each step; all three are exact and need no trial and error, so the workbook does them with ordinary formulas. Deep dive 11.2

Names. The par floating spread is also called the par spread; close relatives are the par asset-swap spread, an FRN's discount margin at par and the CDS par spread. YTM: one all-in yield that mixes rates and credit

The yield to maturity is the single annual rate that, used to discount all of a bond's cash flows, gives back its market price. EUR bonds pay one coupon a year, so it is quoted with annual compounding. Unlike the Z-spread it is an all-in number: it contains the risk-free rate for that maturity and the credit premium together, and nothing in it says which is which. On this case the 10-year YTM is 3.942%; the 10-year swap rate is 3.523%, so the credit part is about 42bp, the same as the 10-year Z-spread. At 1 year the YTM is 3.032% against 3.222% for swaps, about −19bp.

What the model does with it. The fixed loan's price is a rate, so it is compared against a yield. The shortcut reads the YTM at the WAL, interpolating in the same way between the 11-year (3.995%) and 14-year (4.198%) quotes: 4.0627% at 12 years and 4.1303% at 13, so 4.0965% at 12.5. It then restates 9 · Restate that as a half-yearly rate, 2 × (1.040965½ − 1) = 4.0554%, because the loan pays twice a year and the bond once; the fold at the end of stage 2 below explains why and section 11 gives the conversion. Deep dive 11.8 For the calibration the model treats each YTM as the coupon of a bond priced at par, paying annual coupons as this issuer's bonds do, values that bond on the swap curve, and attributes whatever the swap curve cannot explain to default risk. No restatement is needed there, because the bond is valued on its own annual schedule. That is the swap curve's second role, described in section 08. Deep dive 11.4

Z-spread and YTM: two quotes of one bond. Each loan reads its own quote: the floating loan the Z-spread, through the bridge, and the fixed loan the YTM. Taken from one snapshot, both describe the same bond, so the bridge coupon equals the quoted YTM and both give the same default intensities (156.0bp in year 10) and one survival curve 5a · Survival for both loans. Swap curve: the risk-free benchmark, and the two numbers the model builds from it

A par swap rate is the fixed rate at which a plain interest-rate swap, fixed payments against floating 6M EURIBOR, is worth nothing on the day it is struck. The swap curve is that rate for every maturity from 1 to 30 years: here 3.22% at 1 year, rising to 3.61% at 15 and easing to 3.45% at 30 (the swap line on the chart's second tab). It prices time and nothing else. No borrower is involved, so it carries no credit risk, which is why it is the standard stand-in for the risk-free rate.

The model cannot use par rates as they come. A 10-year par rate is an average over ten years: it does not say what a euro paid in year 10 is worth today, nor what the base rate will be in one particular half-year. So the model converts the curve into two kinds of number, and every later use of the curve takes one or the other:

  • Discount factors 10 · Discount, DF(k). What 1 euro paid in k years is worth today. On this curve a euro due in one year is worth 0.9688 (simply 1 ÷ 1.03222, from the 1-year rate of 3.222%) and a euro due in twenty years 0.4952. They are built one year at a time: a par swap is worth nothing when it is struck, so each new maturity's discount factor is whatever makes that true given the ones already found.
  • Forward rates 11 · Forward, f. The base rate the curve implies for one specific half-year. It is just the growth between two neighbouring discount factors: a euro due at 19.5 years is worth 0.5036 and at 20 years 0.4952, so a euro grows by 1.7% over those six months (0.5036 ÷ 0.4952 − 1): a forward rate of 3.38% a year for that half-year.

The two are one piece of information read two ways, since a forward is only a ratio of discount factors. The one input that is not a swap rate is the 6M EURIBOR fixing, today's actual rate for the first six months: the curve starts at 1 year, and the fixing fills in the front end. Section 11 gives the construction and works it through on this curve; section 08 shows where each number is used and what happens if the curve is wrong. Deep dive 11.1

03 · Idea 1 · Quoted spreads are averages

A quoted spread is the average speed of the whole trip

In this sectionInterlude, idea 1 of 2: why the shortcut misses. No stage here; the route resumes at Stage 3 · Default risk (section 06).

Think of a car trip. If you averaged 42 km/h over ten hours of driving, that tells you nothing about how fast you were going in the tenth hour. You may have crawled through traffic early and driven fast at the end.

A bond curve works the same way. The 10-year Z-spread of 42bp is the average credit cost over years 0 to 10. Money that is lent in year 10 pays the cost of year 10 alone, its yearly credit cost (sometimes called the forward credit cost, not to be confused with the forward interest rate of section 02). On a rising curve the yearly cost is far above the average.

Peeling the curve, one year at a time

We can recover each year's own cost from the averages, like peeling an onion from the inside:

  • The 1-year bond covers only year 1, so year 1 costs −19bp.
  • The 2-year bond is the average of years 1 and 2. Year 1 is known, so year 2 follows.
  • Continue out the curve. Each new bond adds exactly one unknown year.

Peeled this way, with every year weighted equally, this is a simple-average approximation. The page's full pricing engine instead runs a full bootstrap 13 · Bootstrap: it solves, year by year, for the default intensity that reprices each bond exactly, with discounting and default risk included. The formula below is the simple-average version, useful for intuition; the table that follows compares it with the full-bootstrap numbers actually used elsewhere on this page. Deep dive 11.4

Trap: weighting the quoted curve flips the sign

It is tempting to skip the peeling step above and weight the quoted curve itself by how much of the loan is outstanding each year. Don't: a quoted point is already an average cost to that maturity, so every long-dated quote has the cheap early years baked into it. Weighting the quotes directly counts those early years again and again, which drags the result down toward the short end.

Done on this case — weighting each quoted level by the loan's own risky-annuity weights (the weights 7 · Weight of section 07) — that gives 17.9 bp, far below the 53.5bp actually charged, making it look as if the borrower overpays by 35.6bp. Peel the years first, as above, and the true fair cost is 60.6bp: the borrower is actually underpaying, by 7.12bp. Same inputs, opposite conclusion.

The fixed case shows the same reversal: weighting the quotes directly gives 3.611%, against the 4.0554% charged — an apparent 44.4bp overpayment — while the correct, peeled calculation gives +7.51bp: an underpayment.

Takeaway

Never price exposure in year t at the curve's quote for maturity t. Convert the curve into the cost of each year first. In this case year 10 alone costs about 96.7 bp, more than twice the 42bp the 10-year quote shows.

04 · Idea 2 · Same WAL is not same timing

WAL measures how much. It does not measure when.

In this sectionInterlude, idea 2 of 2: the same WAL is not the same timing. The route resumes at Stage 3 · Default risk (section 06).

WAL counts principal-years: how much money is lent, for how long. It says nothing about where in time those principal-years sit. Two loans with the same WAL can put their money in very different years, and on a rising curve different years cost different amounts.

Rectangles and triangles

Draw the balance outstanding against time. The area of that shape is the WAL — the same number as the average repayment date less the average drawdown date, for reasons section 05 sets out. Its balance point, the centre of gravity, is something else entirely: the average date at which money is lent out.

  • Bullet maturing at its WAL: the full amount is out from day one to maturity. The shape is a rectangle; it balances in the middle, at WAL/2.
  • Straight amortiser with the same WAL: the balance falls in a straight line to zero. The shape is a triangle. To hold the same area it must run twice as long, and a triangle balances one-third of the way from its tall side, which lands at 2 × WAL/3.

For our loan the centre of gravity is 8.26 years, against 6.25 years for a 12.5-year bullet (ratio 0.661, close to the 2/3 of a pure straight amortiser; the drawdown year and the half-yearly steps shift it slightly). This quantity has no standard name in finance; "exposure-weighted average time" describes it, and its closest relative is Macaulay duration, the textbook centre of gravity of discounted cash flows. Section 05 defines it properly, with diagrams, the formula behind the two numbers above, and the units — this section only needs the fact that the two shapes balance in different places.

Why a straight curve still produces a wedge

The wedge does not depend on the curve bending: a perfectly straight curve produces one too, and the exposure centre 4 · Centre explains why. Two facts combine.

1 · On a straight line, a loan prices at a single point

Averaging a straight line gives the same answer as reading it once, at the average. So a loan's fair cost is simply the cost at its exposure centre: the bullet at WAL/2, the amortiser at 2 × WAL/3. On a rising line the amortiser's later centre means a higher cost — with no curvature anywhere.

2 · The quoted curve rises at half the true rate

A quoted point is an average of every year up to it, and an average always lags what it averages. If the quoted curve rises by g per year, each individual year's cost rises by 2g. The shortcut reads the shallow curve; the loan actually pays the steep one.

How the two facts combine into the rule of thumb

Write the straight curve. Let the quoted curve be quoted(t) = a + g × t: it starts at a and rises g each year. Fact 2 says the per-year costs it averages must rise twice as fast, so cost(t) = a + 2g × t. (Check it: average a + 2g × t from 0 to T and the quoted curve comes back out as a + g × T.)

What the borrower is charged. The shortcut reads the quoted curve at the WAL: charged = a + g × WAL.

What is actually fair. Fact 1 says the loan prices at a single point — its exposure centre E — and pays the per-year cost there: fair = a + 2g × E.

Subtract the two. wedge = (a + 2g × E) − (a + g × WAL) = g × (2E − WAL). The level a cancels out, which is exactly why the rule of thumb depends on the slope alone and not on where the curve sits.

How the shape of the curve sets the size of the wedge: flat, straight and curved, with a sandbox to try it

From there the shape of the curve decides the size:

  • Flat curve: if every year cost the same, timing could not matter and the wedge would be zero. A flat YAS curve comes very close: at 50bp the engine finds a timing effect of −0.07 bp. It is not exactly zero because the gap between the YAS Z-spread and the par floating spread 8a · Par-float widens with maturity (section 02), so flat YAS quotes mean par floating spreads that drift slightly down. The net saving the sandbox shows is −1.18bp, because the shortcut also charges the 50bp YAS figure as a margin, about 1.1bp more than the par floating spread at 12.5 years (the quote-convention effect 1b · Split, section 08).
  • Straight and rising: wedge proportional to the slope.
  • Curved: a curve that flattens at the long end (the usual shape) shrinks the wedge, because the loan's tail lands where costs grow more slowly. A curve that steepens further out enlarges it.
Takeaway

Equal WAL means equal quantity of lending, not equal timing. An amortiser always has its exposure later than the equal-WAL bullet. On any rising curve that alone makes it dearer. The early-year loss and the late-year gain do not cancel, even on a straight line.

05 · Side quest · The two measures of timing

WAL and the exposure centre, defined properly

In this sectionSide quest, optional: the two measures of timing behind idea 2. Skip it and the route continues at Stage 3 · Default risk (section 06).

Two numbers describe when a facility's money is at risk: the WAL and the exposure centre. Both are averages of dates measured in years, both have appeared already, and they are constantly mistaken for one another. Everything from here on rests on telling them apart, so this section defines each one properly, shows what it looks like, and sets them side by side.

WAL, defined two ways

WAL answers one question: how much lending is there, and for how long? There are two routes to it. They look like different quantities — one watches the loan's balance, the other follows individual euros — and they always land on the same number. Both are set out below, then the reason they agree.

Way 1 · by date — watch the balance

Stand at each date in turn and ask how much is still owed at that moment. Multiply by how long that moment lasts, and add up across the life of the loan. In words: how much is out, and for how long, totted up period by period. Geometrically it is the area under the balance-outstanding curve.

2a · WAL-sum
WAL = Σ Oi · a
Oi
The balance outstanding in half-year i, as a fraction of the original principal. It starts at 1 and steps down to 0.
a
The length of each period, 0.5 years. Half-years are used because the loan pays semi-annually.

Units: Oi is a fraction, so fraction × years = years. Note that a division is already hidden inside Oi. Had the balance been written in euros, the sum would come out in euro-years, and you would still have to divide by the principal to get back to years — which is exactly what expressing the balance as a fraction does up front. Deep dive 11.5

Way 2 · by euro — follow the money

Forget the balance and take the euros one at a time. Each euro is drawn on some date and repaid on some later date, and the gap between the two is how long that euro was working. Average that gap over every euro borrowed. In words: the average time a euro stays out.

2 · WAL
WAL = average repayment date − average drawdown date = 12.75 − 0.25 = 12.50
average repayment date
Each repayment date weighted by how much principal comes back on it. Here the 46 instalments are equal and evenly spaced, so their weighted average is simply the midpoint of the first and the last: (1.5 + 24) ÷ 2 = 12.75 years.
average drawdown date
The same calculation on the way in. Here half the money is drawn at signing and half at month 6: (½ × 0) + (½ × 0.5) = 0.25 years. Subtracting it is what stops the loan being credited for time before the money was actually advanced.

Units: years − years = years. Both terms are averages weighted by principal, so each has already been divided by the amount borrowed. When everything is drawn at once on day one the second term is zero and WAL is simply the average repayment date — which is why a bullet's WAL equals its maturity.

Why the two agree

Put side by side, the two ways share one thing: both are totalling euro-years — one euro being outstanding for one year. Way 1 collects euro-years by date, Way 2 collects the same euro-years by euro. Neither can be larger than the other, because they are inventories of the same thing.

That also settles the units. The raw total is genuinely in euro-years: this loan consumes 1bn × 12.5 = 12.5bn euro-years. WAL reports that total per euro borrowed, so the euros cancel and what is left is years — read it as "the average euro stayed out for 12.5 years". Both formulas above have that division already built in, which is why neither shows it explicitly.

To see it, picture the loan as a physical stack of money. Stack the principal up with the slice that will be repaid first on top. As each instalment is paid the stack is shaved off the top, so the outline of the stack over time is exactly the balance-outstanding curve. Shade the whole stack over its life: every square of that shape is one euro-year, and the total area is every euro-year the loan consumes. Sweeping that area vertically is Way 1; sweeping it horizontally is Way 2.

That is the whole reason "average time a euro stays out" and "total principal-years per euro" are one number. The picture also shows, at a glance, why WAL is blind to dispersion: an area can be pushed around in time, stretched late or bunched early, without its total changing at all. Two loans with very different shapes can cover identical area — which is exactly the bullet-versus-amortiser case in section 04.

The exposure centre: where the shape balances

The WAL is the area of that stack of money. The exposure centre asks a different question of the same shape: not how big it is, but where it sits. Imagine cutting the shape out of cardboard and balancing it on a ruler's edge, laid across the time axis. The point where it balances is the exposure centre.

This is worth doing for the two shapes section 04 drew. Both carry the same WAL of 12.5 years — the same area, the same euro-years — so by the WAL they are the same loan. They do not balance in the same place.

The definition · as a sum

Go through the half-years one at a time, note the date, and weight it by how much is still outstanding then. Periods carrying a lot of balance pull the average towards themselves; periods carrying none have no say at all.

The difference from WAL is smaller than it looks. Way 1 added up the euro-years in each half-year, Σ Oi·a, and stopped there — a total, with no mention of when any of it happened. The exposure centre takes those same euro-years and tags each one with its date before averaging. That extra factor, the date, is the whole difference between the two measures.

4 · Centre
exposure centre = Σ (ti − ¼)·Oi·aWAL
ti − ¼
The middle of half-year i, in years from signing. As everywhere on the page, ti = i/2 is the end of half-year i, so its middle is a quarter of a year earlier. The middle rather than the end, because the balance sits there for the whole period, not just at its close.
Oi·a
The euro-years consumed in that half-year: the balance outstanding, as a fraction of principal, times the 0.5-year length of the period. Exactly one column of the stack from Way 1.
WAL
The total euro-years, since that is all the WAL is. Dividing by it is what turns a total into an average.

So the whole thing reads: every euro-year, tagged with the date it happened, divided by all the euro-years. Units: (years × years) ÷ years = years. The euro-years cancel, and with them every trace of how much exposure there is. What survives is purely when it sits — which is why this, and not the WAL, is the number the credit curve prices.

Why the answer is not simply 1. The numerator and the denominator add up the same euro-years, so it is tempting to think they cancel. They do not, because in the numerator each half-year's euro-years are multiplied by their own date before anything is added up. If every half-year had the same date T, it could be taken outside the sum and the result would be T, not 1. Because the dates differ, the result is a weighted average of the dates, in years. It works like an average age: the total of age × headcount, divided by the total headcount, gives the average age, not 1. A loan of 1 outstanding for two half-years shows it:

The same number · as a split

That sum can be rearranged into two pieces that each mean something on their own: a baseline every loan starts from, and a penalty for spreading repayments out in time. It is the clearest statement of what the exposure centre actually measures, and it is what section 04's rule of thumb rests on.

4a · Centre-split
exposure centre = WAL / 2 + (spread of repayment dates)²2 × WAL
WAL / 2
The baseline: where a loan balances if all its exposure is concentrated on a single date. No loan can sit earlier than this.
spread²
The variance of the time each euro stays out, from the day it is drawn to the day it is repaid: spread is the standard deviation of those times, how far they typically sit from their average, the WAL, so squaring it gives years squared. Zero for a bullet, where every euro stays out equally long; large for 46 instalments running over 23 years. (The split is exact when the whole amount is drawn at signing, as in the diagram; a drawdown period moves the centre slightly.)

Units: years + (years² ÷ years) = years. That is what the division by 2 × WAL is for — without it the two terms could not be added. Because a variance can never be negative, the second term only ever pushes the centre later.

Where the split comes from

The split is not a second formula. It is the sum above, rewritten in four steps — and the steps are worth following, because they are also where the lone 2 enters.

  1. Take one euro. A euro drawn at the start and repaid after t years is outstanding on every day in between — one horizontal row of the stack in the first diagram. It therefore contributes t euro-years, and since it is out evenly across that whole span, those euro-years are centred on the middle of the row, at t/2. That halving is the only 2 in the formula.
  2. Weigh the euros against each other. Each euro has its own little balance point at t/2, and the balance point of the whole shape is the average of them. Not a plain average, though: a euro that stayed out for 20 years puts 20 euro-years of weight on the shape, while one that stayed out for 2 years puts only 2. So each euro's centre is weighted by its own euro-years — its t — giving Σ t × (t/2) ÷ Σ t.
  3. Swap the sums for averages. The last step uses a fact about averages, so the two sums need to be averages first. That is easy, because a sum is nothing more than the count times the average: with N euros, Σ t = N × average(t) and Σ t² = N × average(t²). The average time a euro stays out is the WAL (Way 2), so the fraction becomes (½ × N × average(t²)) ÷ (N × WAL) — and the N cancels, top against bottom, leaving average(t²) ÷ (2 × WAL).
  4. Split the average of t². One of the standard identities of statistics says the average of the squares equals the variance plus the square of the average: average(t²) = spread² + WAL². Substituting gives (spread² + WAL²) ÷ (2 × WAL), and separating the two terms leaves WAL² ÷ (2 × WAL), which is just WAL/2, plus spread² ÷ (2 × WAL).

So the 2 is never applied twice. There is one division by 2 × WAL, written first as a single fraction and then shared out between the two terms. Steps 1 and 2 together also explain why dispersion can only push the centre later: a euro that stays out twice as long sits twice as late and carries twice the weight, so its pull on the average grows with the square of its life. Stretching a schedule out therefore moves the centre out more than shortening it moves the centre in.

Closed forms for the two basic shapes

For most schedules the sum above is the only way to get the answer. But for the two shapes in the diagram the answer is known in advance, straight from geometry — a formula that hands you the result rather than making you add up sixty terms. That is what a closed form is.

4b · Centre-shapes
bullet = WAL / 2      straight amortiser = 2 × WAL / 3
bullet
A rectangle balances in the middle. The bullet's exposure is spread evenly from day one to maturity, so it balances at half its length. It is also the split above with a variance of zero: a bullet has a single repayment date, so there is no dispersion to add.
straight amortiser
A triangle balances one third of the way along from its tall side. To hold the same area, the amortiser's triangle has to run twice as long as the bullet, and one third of that double length lands at 2 × WAL / 3.

With WAL = 12.5 years: the bullet balances at 6.25, the amortiser at 8.33. Both figures are marked on the diagram above.

So the three folds above are not three different quantities. The sum is the definition, the split is that same sum rearranged to show what drives it, and the closed forms are available only when the shape is simple enough to recognise. A real schedule — a drawdown ramp, uneven instalments, a grace period — is not a clean triangle, so the model ignores the closed forms and computes the weighted average directly. That is why this loan lands at 8.26 years rather than exactly on the 8.33 the triangle predicts.

The model expresses each year's credit cost as a default intensity λ, also called a hazard rate.

5 · Intensity
λyear b ≈ probability of defaulting during year b, given no default so far

≈ because λ is an intensity: the exact probability is 1 − e−λ, very close to λ when λ is small (worked through in "How survival follows from λ" below).

λ = 1.560% in year 10 means: if the borrower reaches year 10 alive, there is about a 1.6% chance it defaults during that year. The conditional clause is the important part — every λ is quoted on the assumption that the borrower got that far.

Each λ covers a single year in isolation. Pricing needs two further things: the chance of reaching a date at all — the survival probability S(t) = exp(−Σλ), which is what weights every later cash flow — and a way to recover the λs from the bond quotes in the first place. Both are set out below.

How survival follows from λ

Each λ describes one year in isolation. To price anything the model needs the opposite: the chance of reaching a date at all, because a margin due in year 20 is only collected if the borrower is still there in year 20. That number is the survival probability S(t), and it follows from the λs by chaining.

The chaining works precisely because each λ is conditional. Surviving to year 3 means surviving year 1, then surviving year 2 given you survived year 1, then year 3 given the first two. Conditional probabilities multiply, so survival is a running product of one-year survival terms — and a product of exponentials is the exponential of a sum, which is what collapses the chain into a single formula.

5a · Survival
S(t) = exp(−λ1) × exp(−λ2) × … = exp(−Σ λ up to t)
S(t)
The chance of still being alive at t. Starts at 1 and falls as the λs accumulate. Here S(24) = 0.673, so the market implies a 33% chance of default over the loan's life.
exp(−λ)
The chance of getting through one year at intensity λ: 98.452% in year 10. Why the exponential, rather than simply 1 − λ, is worked through below.

Why exp, and not simply 1 − λ

Start with the simplest possible picture. If the borrower could only fail on one date — say the last day of the year — then λ would be that single date's probability, and surviving the year would just be 1 − λ: 98.440% in year 10.

But a borrower can fail on any day. So split the year in two and let each half carry half the intensity. Surviving the year now means surviving both halves, (1 − λ/2) × (1 − λ/2), which comes to 98.446% — close to the first answer, but not the same. Split it into months instead and the answer shifts again. Each extra split spreads the same total intensity over more, smaller chances to fail:

Taking the exponential is just the compact way of saying "default is allowed at any instant, not only on scheduled dates".

The practical gain is consistency. Because the model steps in half-years, it needs a survival figure that does not depend on how the time is chopped up, and only the exponential has that property: exp(−λ/2) applied twice gives back exp(−λ) exactly, whereas (1 − λ/2)² misses 1 − λ by about 0.6bp. Over 48 half-years those small inconsistencies would accumulate.

How each λ is actually found

The credit-margin relation in the first box is a rule of thumb, and the model does not price off it. It recovers the λs from the bond prices themselves, one year at a time.

The condition it imposes is simple: every quoted bond, valued with the model's own λs, must come back to the price the market actually shows. Each maturity's quotes describe the issuer's par bond: the YTM is its coupon, and the YAS Z-spread gives the same coupon through the bridge 12b · Bridge-coupon (sections 02 and 11). So the target price is 100.

Valuing a bond when default is possible

Valuing a bond once default is allowed takes three ingredients, and survival drives two of them:

  • Coupons, which arrive only if the borrower is still alive, so each one is scaled by the survival probability on its date.
  • Recovery, 40% of face, which arrives only if the borrower fails — and the chance of failing in a given half-year is the drop in survival across it.
  • Discounting on the swap curve (section 02), applied to both.
Peeling the curve with the full model

Those three ingredients make the calculation unwind neatly, one year at a time:

  1. The 1-year bond spans only year 1, so its price depends on λ1 and nothing else. One equation, one unknown.
  2. The 2-year bond spans years 1 and 2 — but λ1 is now known, so λ2 is again the only unknown.
  3. Carry on out the curve. Each new bond reaches one year further than the last, so each step adds exactly one new unknown and never more.

This is the same peeling idea as section 03, where each bond's quote was stripped back to the cost of its own final year. What differs is how much each year counts towards the quote it is being stripped out of. Section 03 let every year count the same, which keeps the arithmetic simple enough to follow by hand. Here a year's contribution is discounted, and scaled by the chance the borrower is still alive to reach it, so distant years count for less — which is exactly why the peeled figures there came out below the ones the model actually uses.

Flat λ within each year, and an exact answer with no trial and error

Why can λ be treated as a single number for a whole year? The market supplies one quote per maturity — one figure per year — so there is only enough information to pin down one intensity per year. The model therefore takes λ to be flat within each year and to step at year boundaries. That is an assumption imposed by the data, not something the exponential requires. Count it through: after interpolation the curve supplies one value for each year, so holding λ flat per year leaves exactly one unknown per equation, and the chain has a single answer. Allow λ to move within a year — say a separate value for each half — and that year now carries two unknowns while still offering only one curve value. Twice as many unknowns as equations, and infinitely many λ paths would reprice every bond equally well. Some assumption has to break the tie, and flat-within-the-year is the mildest one available.

With λ flat across the year, both halves of that year carry the same survival factor. Call it u 5b · Step-survival: of the borrowers alive at the start of a half-year, the fraction still alive at its end. Note that u is not S(t), despite both being survival figures. S(t) is a cumulative level — the chance of lasting all the way from signing to t — while u is a single step. S(t) is just every u up to t multiplied together; within year b, S(b) = S(b−1) × u × u.

That is what makes the algebra fall out so neatly. Within the year the bond can default in either half, and it pays its coupon at the year-end (a semi-annual bond would also pay one at the half-year). The half-year is reached with probability u and the year-end with probability u × u. So the unknown never appears in any form more complicated than u and u², which makes the pricing equation an ordinary quadratic — the kind solved with the formula taught at school. Once u is known, λ follows immediately, since u is just exp(−λ/2) seen from the other side.

That is what a closed-form solution means: the answer is produced by evaluating a formula once, rather than being hunted for. The usual alternative in a spreadsheet is Goal Seek, Excel's built-in search: it guesses a λ, reprices the bond, sees how far the price lands from 100, adjusts the guess, and repeats until the gap is small enough to accept. That is iteration — a loop of guess and correct.

Avoiding it is worth more than speed. An iterative search stops at "close enough" rather than exact, can land on slightly different answers depending on where it started and how tight the tolerance was set, and can simply fail to converge on awkward inputs. Solving the quadratic removes all three risks at once: every λ is computed directly, the bonds reprice to their quotes to within about 10−12 bp, and the workbook returns the identical answer every time it is opened — which is what makes the output safe to audit.

Why the recovery assumption itself barely matters

Changing the recovery rate moves the answer only modestly: 7.53 bp at 20% recovery, 7.12 at 40%, 6.36 at 60% (floating loan; section 08). That looks surprising, because R sits inside two formulas on this page — the λ ≈ margin ÷ (1 − R) 3b · Margin-rule rule of thumb above, and the per-default loss, 1 + f·a − R, just above that. Change R and both formulas move. Going in, it changes the λ you calibrate; coming out, it changes the loss that λ is used to price.

Going in: R decides how large λ must be

The quoted spread is fixed market data — it does not move just because you change your assumption about R. But λ ≈ margin ÷ (1 − R) does: raise R and (1 − R) shrinks, so matching that same fixed spread now calls for a larger λ. Credit a default with recovering more, in other words, and the model needs defaults to happen more often to still explain the spread actually observed in the market.

Coming out: R decides how large that default's loss is

That larger λ is now used to price the loan, by way of the loss formula above: λ sets Si−1 − Si, so a bigger λ means a bigger chance of default. R, meanwhile, is not finished — it appears again on its own, inside the per-default loss, 1 + f·a − R, still shrinking what each default costs, exactly as it always did. One assumption about R, acting on the same formula twice: once at a distance, by way of λ, and once directly.

Those two effects sit on different factors of one product, probability of default times per-default loss, so pushing one up and the other down mostly offsets rather than compounding. The offset is not exact — the saving still drifts from 7.53 bp down to 6.36 bp for the floating loan, and from 7.91 to 6.73 bp for the fixed one, across the 20%–60% range. The reason is survival: a higher R calls for a larger λ, so fewer borrowers reach the dear late years, and those years then weigh less in the average. But it is close enough that the recovery rate, within reason, changes the answer only modestly.

The recovery rate — the 40% figure — is a soft assumption: it enters the calculation twice, going in and coming out, in ways that offset, so the model absorbs most of whatever rate you choose. The recovery base — original face or outstanding balance, from the subsection above — is not soft at all; it enters only once, with no offsetting error to cancel it. A wrong base does not crash the model or produce an obviously broken number: it quietly makes the late years price as nearly free, by turning their loss given default negative. Sections 03–05 found the wedge precisely in those late years — so a base error would not just bias this section's output, it would erase the thing the whole page is about.

Two parts of the saving

The saving compares the fair price with what the shortcut charges, and the two differ for two separate reasons. The WAL timing effect is the wedge this page is about: the model's price of the loan against its price of a bullet with the same WAL, both priced on the same default intensities. Why there is a risk-free effect at all

The 12.5-year par yield is itself an average of forwards: those of a riskless bond that lends from today to 12.5 years and stops. The loan lends in the same early years, but it also keeps 15% of its weight beyond 12.5 years, and the forwards there are not the same as before it. On this swap curve, which peaks at 15 years and falls to 3.45% at 30, most of that late weight lands in years 12–18, where forwards are slightly higher (3.68%), but some lands in years 18–24, where they have fallen to 3.12% (section 07's four blocks). The saving shown, +0.40bp, is the quote-convention effect: the shortcut reads the 1-year YAS Z-spread (held flat below 1 year), which is not the par floating spread of a six-month bullet; the model also prices the first six months off the 6M fixing. On the fixed loan the same effect is large, −13.4bp: the shortcut charges the 1-year YTM, restated half-yearly, while a six-month loan's base rate is the 6M fixing, well below the 1-year rate.

Try "Inverted". Who benefits now?

The lender. Late years are now cheaper than the WAL point, so the shortcut overcharges and the saving turns negative. The workbook reports it as negative and never floors it at zero.

Keep 23 years of repayment and lengthen the drawdown period to 3 and then 5 years. What happens?

The floating saving roughly doubles and then triples: 7.12bp with 1 year of drawdown, 15.11bp with 3 years, 22.53bp with 5 years (PV €6.4m, €13.8m, €20.7m on EUR 1bn). The shortcut point moves only from 12.5 to 14.5 years, but the loan's tail moves into years 24–28, where this curve implies its highest yearly costs (λ of about 260–290bp in years 23–25). A small move in WAL hides a large move in where the risk sits. Deep dive 11.5

Takeaway

The answer moves with the shape of the bond curves and the shape of the loan: how steep the curve is, where it bends, and how far the loan reaches into the tail. It moves very little with the supporting inputs.

The risk-free curve: discount factors, forwards and rates

↑ Belongs to the main route: Stage 1 · The market data

Purpose. The swap curve is the model's risk-free benchmark (section 02). It comes in as par swap rates, one per year. Pricing needs the same curve in other forms: what a euro paid on a given date is worth today, the base rate of each half-year, and, for comparisons, one rate for a given maturity. This block builds all of them from the one input and says what each one is for.

Intuition. One curve, read several ways. A par swap rate is an average over a whole stream of payments up to its maturity. A forward is the rate for one half-year. A discount factor is the combined effect of all the forwards up to one date. They hold the same information; each answers a different question, and the model uses whichever form the question needs.

1 · Discount factors: what a euro on each date is worth today

In plain words. A discount factor is a price tag on a future euro. DF(1) = 0.9688 means: put 0.9688 euros on deposit today at the 1-year rate of 3.222% and you have exactly 1 euro in a year. A euro due in twenty years costs 0.4952 today. Why the model needs it. Every present value on this page is a sum of amounts times discount factors, and each half-year's weight 7 · Weight starts from one.

How the model gets them from swap rates: one year at a time. Year 1 is direct: a euro in a year costs 1 ÷ 1.03222 = 0.9688 today. For year 2, the 2-year swap rate, 3.3919%, behaves like the coupon of a riskless 2-year bond worth exactly 100%: it pays 3.3919% after one year and 103.3919% after two. The first payment is valued with the DF(1) already known, so the only unknown left is DF(2), and it is whatever makes the bond worth exactly 100%: 0.9354. Year 3 then uses DF(1) and DF(2), and so on up to 30 years. The formula below is that one step written once for every year. (The λ bootstrap of 11.4 peels the credit curve in the same way.)

It replaces the interpolated DF(½) and nothing else: DF(1) is still fixed by the 1-year swap rate, so the fixing does not change what a euro in one year is worth. Insight. Discount factors are the master numbers: every other reading in this block is calculated from them. A swap curve or a 6M fixing from the wrong day therefore reaches the whole model through them (section 08, "How robust is the number?").

2 · Forwards: the base rate of each half-year

In plain words. The forward is the 6-month interest rate the swap curve lets you lock in today for one particular future half-year, say the one between 19.5 and 20 years. Why the model needs it. It is what a floating borrower pays each half-year on top of the margin, it sets the interest lost in a default 6 · Loss, and it is the risk-free part of a fixed loan's cost 3c · Fixed-cost.

Intuition. A euro due at 19.5 years is worth 0.5036 today and a euro due at 20 years 0.4952. Waiting the extra six months is therefore worth 0.5036 ÷ 0.4952 − 1 = 1.69%: that is the half-year's forward, 3.38% a year.

Insight. Forwards are the marginal rates and par swap rates the averages, so they behave like a marginal and an average cost. Where forwards sit above the par rate, par rates are still rising; where they drop below it, par rates start to fall. On this curve the forwards are about 3.93% just before 15 years, above the 3.61% par rate, then drop to 3.39% straight after and to 2.60% by 24 years: that is why par rates peak at 15 years. A loan that lends beyond 15 years reaches cheaper forwards than its WAL suggests, which gives the fixed loan its small negative risk-free effect (section 08; 11.9).

Purpose. The floating loan's input is a YAS Z-spread, but every floating price the model produces is a par floating spread. This block defines both, explains why one cannot simply be relabelled as the other, and builds the conversion step by step, with the 20-year bond worked through.

You cannot get from 78bp to 76.0bp by a simple adjustment, because the two numbers measure the credit in different ways. The YAS Z-spread is one extra discount rate applied to a fixed-coupon bond: it says how much to mark down the bond's payments, and nothing about how likely default is or how much would be recovered. The bridge connects two different ways of stating the same issuer's credit risk. Open each one for its definition, formula and intuition.

The starting point: the YAS Z-spread

First, the zero rate. The Z-spread is added to the swap curve's zero rate zt of each payment date 10d · Zero: the single rate that discounts one payment due at t, built in 11.1 from the swap discount factors. On this curve z20 = 3.5762%.

Definition. The YAS Z-spread of a bond is the single number Z that, added to the zero rate of every payment date, makes the bond's promised payments, discounted at those raised rates, add up to its market price. For a bond paying an annual coupon c and maturing at T:

price = Σt=1…T c(1 + zt + Z)t + 1(1 + zT + Z)T12 · Z-spread
c
The coupon, paid once a year on each date t = 1, 2, …, T.
1

Z
The one number added to every date's zero rate. Bloomberg finds it by trial and error from the bond's market price.

Intuition. Discounted at the swap zero rates alone, the bond would be worth what a risk-free bond with the same payments is worth. The issuer's bond is worth less, and YAS expresses that shortfall in the bluntest way available: it assumes every payment will be made in full and simply discounts each one more heavily. It never asks how likely a default is, or what would be recovered. That is what "no view on default" means: all the credit risk is packed into the one extra rate Z.

The destination: the par floating spread

Definition. The par floating spread sT is the margin over the 6M forward at which a half-yearly floating bond of the issuer, maturing at T, is worth exactly 100, when its coupons are paid only while the issuer survives and a share R of face is recovered on default. R is the recovery rate used throughout the page (Inputs row 13), 40% in this case.

Where the formula comes from. Split the floating bond's payments into two parts and value each one:

  • The base-rate part: the 6M forward on every coupon date, plus the principal at T. With no default risk this part alone would be worth exactly 100, which is precisely what the forwards are built to do (11.1: a riskless floater prices at par). With default risk it is worth less. The shortfall is the present value of the expected loss 6 · Loss: in each half-year, the chance of default times what would be lost (principal plus that half-year's interest, less the recovery), discounted to today.
  • The margin part: the margin sT, paid as sT × a every half-year while the issuer survives. It is worth sT × the risky annuity 7a · Annuity, the value of receiving a margin of 1 in that way (defined below).

The bond is worth 100 when the margin part makes up the shortfall exactly: (100 − PV of expected loss) + sT × risky annuity = 100. Solving for sT:

Step 1 · Rebuild the bond the Z-spread describes

Intuition. On its own a Z-spread is not a price, only a way of discounting. To make it concrete, ask which bond it describes, and pick the simplest one: the par bond, priced at exactly 100. Its price is known (100) and its discounting is known (zero rates plus Z), so the only unknown left in the YAS equation is the coupon. And the coupon enters that equation in a straight line, so it can be solved for directly, with no trial and error.

Step 2 · Turn the bond's price into default risk

Intuition. Valued on the swap curve alone, as if it could never default, the 4.349% bond would be worth 111.0. The market prices it at 100. The 11.0 gap is what default risk takes away: coupons and principal that may never be paid, partly made good by the 40% recovery. Step 2 finds, year by year, the default intensity λ that takes away exactly that gap and no more.

How. This is the bootstrap of the block "Bootstrapping λ in closed form" below: the bond paying cT is priced with survival and recovery, and λ is solved one year at a time so that it is worth 100. On this case survival to year 20 comes out at 0.7454, a 25.5% chance of default within twenty years. The fixed loan runs the same bootstrap with the YTM as the coupon; since cT equals the YTM, the two give one and the same λ.

Step 3 · Re-express the default risk as a margin

With survival known for every half-year, the par floating spread formula above can be evaluated directly. At 20 years the present value of the expected losses is 0.1001 per unit of face and the risky annuity 13.173 years, so s20 = 0.1001 ÷ 13.173 = 75.95bp.

The model never needs this number for its own sake: it carries λ forward, not sT. The floating loan's fair margin in section 07 is the same calculation applied to the loan's balance profile instead of a bullet. Step 3 is shown here only to make the comparison visible: the same bond reads 78bp as a YAS Z-spread and 76.0bp as a par floating spread. The gap grows with maturity (6.0 against 5.8bp at 5 years, 42.0 against 41.0bp at 10), for the reasons given in section 02: the margin is paid half-yearly and the spread compounded yearly, a margin is collected only while the issuer survives, and the floating bond recovers 40% on default.

Bootstrapping λ in closed form

↑ Belongs to the main route: Stage 3 · Default risk

Purpose. Find the one number — λb, the default intensity for year b — that makes the quoted bond maturing at b worth exactly its market price. Years 1…b−1 are already solved, so λb is the only unknown. Within year b it is held constant (section 06), so both half-years share one survival factor: u = e−λb/2, the fraction surviving six months.

Intuition. Each quoted bond's price (100) and coupons are known; what is not yet known is how likely the borrower is to survive the bond's final year, because every earlier year has already been fixed by a shorter bond. So the curve is peeled one year at a time: the 1-year bond fixes year 1, the 2-year bond then fixes year 2, and so on. Within a year, survival enters the price only as u (to the half-year) and u² (to the year-end), so each step is an ordinary quadratic, solved by the school formula with no trial and error.

Formulas, parameters and worked examples

The equation. Value the bond, set it equal to its price, and solve. The bond is the issuer's par bond paying the coupon cb: the YTM for the fixed loan, the bridge coupon for the floating one. Its price is 1 per unit of face — which is the left-hand side:

1 = K + DF(b−½)·Sp·[u·g1 + R(1−u)] + DF(b)·Sp·[u²·g2 + R(u − u²)]13 · Bootstrap
1
The bond's price. It is 1 because each maturity's quotes describe a par bond; repricing to par is the condition being imposed.
K
Everything the bond pays before year b, already valued because those λs are known: Σ DF[S·coupon + R·ΔS], where ΔS is the fall in survival across a period — the share of scenarios in which default happens and recovery is paid instead. Coupons are cb/m, paid on the bond's own coupon dates (once a year here, at year ends), and recovery R on default in any half-year.
first bracket
The payment at b−½, discounted by DF(b−½). Two outcomes: survive that half-year, with probability u, and collect g1; or default in it, with probability 1−u, and collect recovery R. g1 = cb/2 for a semi-annual bond and 0 for an annual one, which pays nothing mid-year.
second bracket
The payment at b, discounted by DF(b). Survive both halves, probability u², and collect g2 = cb/m + 1 — the year-end coupon plus the principal; or get through the first half and fail in the second, probability u − u², and collect R.
Sp
Survival to the start of year b. Both brackets sit behind it because none of it happens unless the borrower reaches year b at all.

The ordinary discount factors of 11.1, read at this year's two payment dates — the same quantity written DFi in the weights later on. For year 1 that means DF(0.5) = 0.984926 and DF(1) = 0.968786.

So the whole right-hand side reads: what the bond has already paid, plus what it pays in its final year under each of the three ways that year can go — survive both halves, fail in the first, fail in the second.

The cost of a half-year

↑ Belongs to the main route: Stage 5 · The cost of each half-year

Purpose. Restate the risk of loss in each half-year as a margin: the break-even margin a lender would need for that half-year alone. These costs, one per half-year, are what the fair price averages (11.7). Comparing them with the one flat price the shortcut charges is the whole wedge.

In plain words. Think of one half-year of lending on its own. In the half-year ending at year 20, the borrower defaults with a chance of 1.12%, and the lender then loses 61.7% of the balance: the principal plus the half-year's interest, less the 40% recovered. That is an expected loss of 0.69%. A margin, though, is collected only if the borrower survives (98.88%), and only for half a year. To break even it must therefore be 0.69% ÷ (98.88% × 0.5) = 1.40% a year: 140bp.

Weight, fair price and present value

↑ Belongs to the main route: Stage 6 and Stage 7 · The weight of each half-year / The fair price

Purpose. Combine the half-year costs of 11.6 into one fair margin or rate for the whole loan, the model's own price for it, and turn its gap to what is charged into euros. It answers two questions: what single price is fair for this exact profile, and what is the saving worth today?

In plain words. A flat margin earns more in some half-years than in others: more where more money is outstanding, sooner rather than later, and only while the borrower is still there. The weight of a half-year measures exactly that: what 1bp of margin in it is worth today. The fair price is the one flat margin whose value equals the value of all the half-year costs, which makes it a weighted average of those costs, with these weights.

Insight. Early half-years carry far more weight than late ones, because more is lent and less is discounted, but the late ones cost far more. The fair price is the balance between the two: on this loan the dear late years win, which is the saving (section 07).

The fixed saving, a second view: rates part and credit part

↑ Belongs to the main route: Stage 8 · The saving and where it comes from

Purpose. Look at the fixed loan's saving of 7.51bp a year from a second angle. 11.8 splits it by cause: the loan's shape (+7.71bp) and how the quote is read (−0.20bp). This card splits the same 7.51bp by ingredient: how much comes from the swap curve and how much from credit. The two behave very differently. The credit part reflects the borrower's own risk, much like the floating saving. The swap-curve part depends only on the shape of the swap curve, is the same for any borrower, and can work for or against them. It applies to the fixed loan only: a floating borrower pays the swap rate as it resets, so there is no risk-free part to misprice.

In plain words. A fixed rate is two prices in one: the cost of money over the period lent (the risk-free part) and a charge for the borrower's credit. Both the fair rate and the shortcut contain the two. Split each into its two parts and compare like with like: risk-free against risk-free, credit against credit. The gap in each pair is one effect.

How the two views fit together. They are not two steps but two cuts of the same total, like a sales figure split by region or by product. The loan's shape, which 11.8 measures as the timing effect, shows up here inside each ingredient: it makes the swap-curve part slightly negative and the credit part large and positive, as the next paragraph explains. The −0.20bp convention effect of 11.8 ends up mostly in the credit part. The match is not exact, because the two cards use slightly different 12.5-year references (11.8 the model's risky bullet, this card a riskless one), so the pieces of one view cannot be read off the other.

Where the risk-free effect comes from. The shortcut's risk-free part is the 12.5-year swap rate. As 11.1 shows, a swap rate is an average of the six-month rates up to its maturity, so it averages the swap curve over years 0 to 12.5 only. The loan keeps lending until year 24, so its own risk-free part averages the same curve over years 0 to 24, counting most where most is lent. On this curve the years after 12.5 are cheaper overall, so the loan's average is a little lower: −0.85bp. The credit side follows the same logic, but the credit curve keeps rising, so the loan's average credit cost is well above what the shortcut charges: +8.36bp.

12 · Questions people ask

Questions and answers

What if the bond curve is flat? What if it is straight?

Flat: every year costs the same, so it does not matter when the money is lent and the WAL timing effect 1b · Split is about zero (−0.07bp for a flat 50bp YAS curve). The net saving is then the quote-convention effect, about −1.1bp, because the shortcut charges a YAS Z-spread as if it were a margin (section 08). Straight and rising: there is a saving roughly equal to slope × WAL ÷ 3 1a · Rule, because the amortiser's exposure sits later than the bullet's. Curvature then adds (steepening) or subtracts (flattening). See section 04.

Isn't the WAL already a centre of gravity? Why is it different?

For a loan drawn in full at signing, WAL is the centre of gravity of the repayments (with a drawdown period, less the average drawdown date). The credit curve prices the balance outstanding, whose centre of gravity is WAL/2 for a bullet and about 2·WAL/3 for a straight amortiser (8.26 years for this loan vs 6.25 for the bullet). There is no standard name; "exposure-weighted average time" describes it. Macaulay duration is the textbook relative.

Does the model recompute a centre of gravity for every half-year?

No. The centre of gravity is a teaching device and is exact only for straight curves. The model never computes it. It takes each half-year's balance (from the schedule, computed once), each half-year's cost 3 · Cost (from the curve) and each half-year's weight, and averages. That works for any curve shape.

Where do the "share of weight" percentages come from?

Each half-year's weight 7 · Weight is balance × 0.5 × discount factor × survival. A block's share is its weights divided by the total, 8.973. The 18–24 year block holds 6.8% of the principal-years but only 3.4% of the weight, because those euros are far in the future and only collected if the borrower survives. The share is not multiplied by the balance again, and the block's cost (143.6 bp) comes from the curve, not from the loan.

Which "spread" drives λ: the Z-spread or the whole YTM?

Both, each for its own loan, and on one consistent snapshot they agree. Floating: the YAS Z-spread, turned by the bridge 12b · Bridge-coupon into the coupon of the par bond it describes (section 02). Fixed: the YTM, which already is that coupon. The model then values the bond on the swap curve and attributes whatever the curve cannot explain to λ. At 1 year: YTM 3.032% against swap 3.222% gives about −19bp of credit, the same as the 1-year Z-spread; the rule of thumb λ ≈ margin ÷ (1 − R) 3b · Margin-rule turns that into about −32bp of λ, close to the −30bp both calibrations find for year 1.

Is λ a real probability of default?

No, it is market-implied. It includes everything investors charge for, not only expected losses, so it is usually well above historical default rates, and here it is even negative at the short end. It is the right input for pricing consistently with the borrower's bonds, and the wrong input for a credit risk forecast.

Why does the workbook warn "Negative forward hazard"?

Because the short bonds trade below swaps. A bond can only be richer than the risk-free curve if the implied default intensity 5 · Intensity is negative. The pricing remains internally consistent; the flag asks you to confirm the data (same swap curve as the Z-spreads, same snapshot; Results row 55 checks it).

Why is row 12 different from row 11 when the margin X is zero?

Floating: it isn't; 53.5 bp and 0.5350% are the same number. Fixed: the annual-coupon bond yield (4.0965%) is restated 9 · Restate as the half-yearly rate with the same effective yield (4.0554%), so it can be compared with the fair rate, which is computed on the loan's half-yearly payments.

What is the risky annuity in row 9?

The sum of all half-year weights: the loan's principal-years after discounting and default risk. It converts basis points into today's money: 1bp a year on this loan is worth 1bp × 8.973 × EUR 1bn ≈ €0.9m. In CDS markets the same quantity is called the risky PV01 (RPV01).

What is the 6M fixing for?

The swap curve starts at 1 year, but the model needs a discount factor every six months. The 6M EURIBOR fixing gives the actual rate for the first six months instead of interpolating between today and the 1-year point. For a floating loan it barely matters (0.002bp here). For a fixed loan it does: it pins the first half-year's forward, which is part of the fixed rate, and leaving it out moves the fixed saving by about 0.4bp (7.51 to 7.15). Enter the real fixing, from the same snapshot as the rest of the curve.

How much does the swap curve matter? Can we use free data?

Its level matters little: a 30bp error in the whole curve, 6M fixing included, moves the floating saving from 7.12 to 6.89bp and the fixed from 7.51 to 7.75bp (section 08). What matters is consistency: the curve must be the one YAS measures the Z-spreads against, from the same snapshot as the bond quotes. Free sources come from another source and another moment, so use them only for indicative work and expect the consistency check 12c · Gap (Results row 55) to flag them; in production take everything from one Bloomberg snapshot (Data Sourcing tab). The bond curves are what drive the answer.

Why are the loss and the benefit not equal on a straight curve?

Because the loan's exposure centre 4 · Centre is later than the equal-WAL bullet's. The benefit side reaches further up the curve than the loss side reaches down it. See the rectangle-and-triangle figure in section 04.

Why is the fixed "risk-free effect" negative here?

The swap curve rises to 3.61% at 15 years and then falls to 3.45% at 30. Risk-free forwards in the loan's late years are therefore below the 12.5-year par rate. Reading the fixed rate at 12.5 years slightly overstates the risk-free part for this profile (−0.85 bp), while the credit part is understated by 8.36 bp. Net, the borrower still saves 7.51 bp.

Does the lender lose money?

Not in an accounting sense. The saving is margin the lender forgoes relative to pricing the loan consistently with the borrower's bond curve. Whether that is acceptable depends on relationship, ancillary business, funding and capital, none of which this calculator models. Use it to know the price of the convention, then decide.

What does recovery change?

Not much: from 7.53 bp (20%) to 6.36 bp (60%) on the floating side, and from 7.91 to 6.73 bp on the fixed side. A higher recovery raises the implied λ and lowers the loss per default, which mostly offsets. What remains comes from survival: a larger λ means fewer borrowers reach the dear late years, so they weigh less in the average. What matters is applying recovery to the balance outstanding, not to the original amount.

Assumptions and limitations

  • Bond quotes as par bonds. Each maturity's YAS Z-spread and YTM are read as the issuer's par bond at a whole-year maturity, paying coupons at the frequency in Inputs row 17. Exact for a fitted par curve (Bloomberg CRVF / BVAL); for individual bonds far from par each spread level shifts by a few bp, because recovery is paid on face. The consistency check 12c · Gap cannot see this, since Z-spread and YTM shift together, so prefer near-par bonds.
  • One snapshot, default YAS settings. Z-spreads, YTMs, swap curve and fixing must come from one Bloomberg snapshot, and the swap curve must be the one YAS measures the Z-spreads against, with YAS left at its default compounding (the coupon frequency). The workbook flags gaps above 1bp (Results rows 54–55).
  • Loan mechanics. Semi-annual periods, accrual 0.5; floating resets to the 6M forward. Interest on drawn balances only; no commitment fee unless entered as a fee. Drawdowns are equal half-yearly amounts from signing.
  • Fixed rate basis. The charged fixed rate is the YTM at the WAL restated on a semi-annual basis, plus X. If the term sheet uses the annual number as a half-yearly coupon, add the difference through X.
  • Single curve. The same swap curve projects and discounts. No basis between loan and bond funding, no bond new-issue premium unless entered (Inputs rows 22–24).
  • One intensity per year. λ is held flat within each year and steps at year boundaries, because the curve supplies one quote per maturity and so identifies one intensity per year. A curve that is flat in par floating spreads 8a · Par-float therefore still leaves a tiny residue rather than exactly zero.
  • Beyond the data. Inside the quotes, years are interpolated linearly; beyond the last bond quote the last λ is extended and flagged. Maximum life 30 years.
  • Validation. Four implementations agree on this case to every digit shown: the workbook, its Python mirror (wal_wedge_engine.py, in the same folder), and the engines inside this page and the borrower view. On every run the workbook checks that each quoted bond reprices to its quote (to about 10−12bp), that the PV identity holds (to about 10−7 euros) and that the two WAL formulas agree (Results rows 43–46). The engine was first validated against the original reference specification, before the YAS bridge was introduced; the workbook's Instructions tab records both stages.

Glossary

WALWeighted average life: average repayment date minus average drawdown date. Measures the quantity of lending.
Exposure centreAverage date at which money is outstanding. WAL/2 for a bullet, about 2·WAL/3 for a straight amortiser.
Spot / quoted spreadSpread of a bond maturing at T: an average of the credit cost over years 0 to T.
Yearly credit costThe credit cost of one specific year, peeled from the quoted averages (section 03). What actually prices money lent in that year. Sometimes called the forward credit cost.
Forward rateThe risk-free six-month rate the swap curve implies for one future half-year (sections 02 and 11.1). Not the same thing as the yearly credit cost.
λ (default intensity)Default intensity: close to the market-implied probability of defaulting in a year, given survival so far (exactly 1 − e−λ). Section 06.
Survival S(t)Market-implied probability of no default by t.
Risky annuityPV of 1 per year on the loan's balance, allowing for discounting and default. Converts bp into money.
Weight (of a half-year)How much a half-year counts when the 48 half-year costs are averaged into the fair price: balance outstanding × 0.5 × discount factor × survival. The weights add up to the risky annuity. Section 07.
Swap curvePar swap rates for 1 to 30 years: the market's risk-free benchmark. Converted into discount factors (DF) and half-year forward rates (f), which are the only two things the model takes from it. Section 02.
ShortcutPricing the loan at the bond curve read at its WAL.
YAS Z-spreadBloomberg's Z-spread: the extra discount rate, added to every swap zero rate, that discounts a bond's promised cash flows to its price. The floating loan's input. Section 02.
Par floating spreadThe margin over the 6M rate at which a six-monthly floating bond of the issuer would be worth 100, given default and recovery. The unit of every floating price the model outputs. Section 02.
YAS bridgeThe three-step conversion from a YAS Z-spread to a par floating spread, through the par bond the Z-spread describes and the default risk that explains its price. Sections 02 and 11.2.
WAL timing / quote-convention effectThe two parts of the net saving: the model's price of the loan against its price of a bullet at the WAL (timing), and that bullet against the shortcut, a market quote (convention). Sections 08 and 11.8, Results rows 56–57.
