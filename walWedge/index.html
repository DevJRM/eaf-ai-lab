01 · The question in plain words

Two ways to borrow EUR 5bn, one pricing habit

A borrower can raise EUR 5bn by issuing bonds or by taking an amortising loan. Lenders usually price the loan off the borrower's own bond curve, because that curve is the market's live view of the borrower's credit. The question is which point on that curve to use.

A bond repays everything at maturity. Our loan repays a little every six months for 23 years, so a lot of the money comes back early. The usual habit is to summarise the loan by its weighted average life (WAL), here 12.5 years, and read the bond curve at 12.5 years. We call this the shortcut.

The shortcut says

"This loan behaves like a 12.5-year bond, so charge the 12.5-year spread."

Z-spread at 12.5y = 53.5 bp · YTM at 12.5y = 4.0965% annual = 4.0554% half-yearly

The exact answer says

"Price every half-year of the loan at what that half-year actually costs on the bond curve, then average."

Fair spread = 62.3 bp · fair fixed rate = 4.1355% half-yearly

Both fixed rates above are quoted on the loan's own half-yearly basis, so they can be compared directly: 4.1355 − 4.0554 = 8.0 bp. EUR bonds quote an annual yield, and 4.0965% annual is the same yield as 4.0554% paid half-yearly; section 07 shows the conversion and why it matters.

The difference between the two is the wedge. When it is positive, the shortcut charges less than the borrower's own bond market would, so the borrower saves and the lender gives up margin relative to bond-equivalent pricing. When it is negative, the borrower overpays.

wedge = fair spread or rate − shortcut spread or rate (+ X)
fair
What this exact drawdown and repayment profile costs on the borrower's bond curve. Results row 13.
shortcut
The curve read at the WAL, plus any agreed margin X above or below it. Results rows 11–12.
Takeaway

The saving is a net figure. Before the WAL date the borrower overpays, because early exposure is genuinely cheaper than the 12.5-year point. After the WAL date it underpays, because late exposure is much dearer. On a rising curve the second effect wins. Sections 03 and 04 explain why it wins even when the curve is a straight line.

03 · Idea 1 · Quoted spreads are averages

A quoted spread is the average speed of the whole trip

Think of a car trip. If you averaged 42 km/h over ten hours of driving, that tells you nothing about how fast you were going in the tenth hour. You may have crawled through traffic early and driven fast at the end.

A bond curve works the same way. The 10-year Z-spread of 42bp is the average credit cost over years 0 to 10. Money that is lent in year 10 pays the cost of year 10 alone, which is called the forward cost. On a rising curve the forward cost is far above the average.

Peeling the curve, one year at a time

We can recover each year's own cost from the averages, like peeling an onion from the inside:

  • The 1-year bond covers only year 1, so year 1 costs −19bp.
  • The 2-year bond is the average of years 1 and 2. Year 1 is known, so year 2 follows.
  • Continue out the curve. Each new bond adds exactly one unknown year.

Peeled this way, with every year weighted equally, this is a simple-average approximation. The page's full pricing engine instead runs a full bootstrap: it solves, year by year, for the default intensity that reprices each bond exactly, with discounting and default risk included. The formula below is the simple-average version, useful for intuition; the table that follows compares it with the full-bootstrap numbers actually used elsewhere on this page.

cost of year b ≈ b × spread(b) − (b−1) × spread(b−1)
b × spread(b)
Total credit cost of years 1 to b, if each year counted equally.
(b−1) × spread(b−1)
Total cost of years 1 to b−1, already known.

This simple-average approximation treats every year as equally important. The full bootstrap also discounts later years and allows for default, so later years count less; a later year then needs a higher cost to move the average by the same amount. That is why the full-bootstrap figures below are a bit higher than the simple-average ones.

Trap: weighting the quoted curve flips the sign

It is tempting to skip the peeling step above and weight the quoted curve itself by how much of the loan is outstanding each year. Don't: a quoted point is already an average cost to that maturity, so every long-dated quote has the cheap early years baked into it. Weighting the quotes directly counts those early years again and again, which drags the result down toward the short end.

Done on this case — weighting each quoted level by the loan's own risky-annuity weights — that gives 17.9 bp, far below the 53.5bp actually charged, making it look as if the borrower overpays by 35.6bp. Peel the years first, as above, and the true fair cost is 62.3bp: the borrower is actually underpaying, by 8.82bp. Same inputs, opposite conclusion.

The fixed case shows the same reversal: weighting the quotes directly gives 3.607%, against the 4.0554% charged — an apparent 44.9bp overpayment — while the correct, peeled calculation gives +8.02bp: an underpayment.

Takeaway

Never price exposure in year t at the curve's quote for maturity t. Convert the curve into the cost of each year first. In this case year 10 alone costs about 99.0 bp, more than twice the 42bp the 10-year quote shows.

04 · Idea 2 · Same WAL is not same timing

WAL measures how much. It does not measure when.

WAL counts principal-years: how much money is lent, for how long. It says nothing about where in time those principal-years sit. Two loans with the same WAL can put their money in very different years, and on a rising curve different years cost different amounts.

Rectangles and triangles

Draw the balance outstanding against time. The area of that shape is the WAL — the same number as the average repayment date, for reasons section 05 sets out. Its balance point, the centre of gravity, is something else entirely: the average date at which money is lent out.

  • Bullet maturing at its WAL: the full amount is out from day one to maturity. The shape is a rectangle; it balances in the middle, at WAL/2.
  • Straight amortiser with the same WAL: the balance falls in a straight line to zero. The shape is a triangle. To hold the same area it must run twice as long, and a triangle balances one-third of the way from its tall side, which lands at 2 × WAL/3.

For our loan the centre of gravity is 8.26 years, against 6.25 years for a 12.5-year bullet (ratio 0.661, close to the 2/3 of a pure straight amortiser; the drawdown year and the half-yearly steps shift it slightly). This quantity has no standard name in finance; "exposure-weighted average time" describes it, and its closest relative is Macaulay duration, the textbook centre of gravity of discounted cash flows. Section 05 defines it properly, with diagrams, the formula behind the two numbers above, and the units — this section only needs the fact that the two shapes balance in different places.

Why a straight curve still produces a wedge

It is natural to assume the wedge needs a curved curve. It does not. A perfectly straight curve produces one too, and the exposure centre explains why. Two facts combine.

1 · On a straight line, a loan prices at a single point

Averaging a straight line gives the same answer as reading it once, at the average. So a loan's fair cost is simply the cost at its exposure centre: the bullet at WAL/2, the amortiser at 2 × WAL/3. On a rising line the amortiser's later centre means a higher cost — with no curvature anywhere.

2 · The quoted curve rises at half the true rate

A quoted point is an average of every year up to it, and an average always lags what it averages. If the quoted curve rises by g per year, each individual year's cost rises by 2g. The shortcut reads the shallow curve; the loan actually pays the steep one.

wedge ≈ quoted-curve slope g × WAL ÷ 3
g
How fast the quoted curve rises, in bp per year.
÷ 3
Carries the dispersion of a straight amortiser's repayments. A bullet has no dispersion, so its wedge is exactly zero: the shortcut prices it correctly.

For a curve of 10bp rising 4bp a year and our loan: 4 × 12.5 ÷ 3 = 16.7 bp, against the engine's exact 13.58 bp. The rule of thumb counts every year equally; the engine discounts later years and allows for default, which gives the long tail less weight and brings the figure down.

How the two facts combine into the rule of thumb

Write the straight curve. Let the quoted curve be quoted(t) = a + g × t: it starts at a and rises g each year. Fact 2 says the per-year costs it averages must rise twice as fast, so cost(t) = a + 2g × t. (Check it: average a + 2g × t from 0 to T and the quoted curve comes back out as a + g × T.)

What the borrower is charged. The shortcut reads the quoted curve at the WAL: charged = a + g × WAL.

What is actually fair. Fact 1 says the loan prices at a single point — its exposure centre E — and pays the per-year cost there: fair = a + 2g × E.

Subtract the two. wedge = (a + 2g × E) − (a + g × WAL) = g × (2E − WAL). The level a cancels out, which is exactly why the rule of thumb depends on the slope alone and not on where the curve sits.

That leaves one number to find per shape, and both were derived earlier:

ShapeExposure centre E2E − WALWedge

Straight amortiser2 × WAL / 3WAL / 3g × WAL / 3

So the ÷ 3 is simply 2 × (2/3) − 1: what is left after doubling the amortiser's exposure centre and taking off the WAL the shortcut charges at. A bullet sits exactly at WAL/2, so the two prices meet and its wedge is zero. Every amortiser sits beyond WAL/2, so every amortiser has a positive wedge.

From there the shape of the curve decides the size:

  • Flat curve: every year costs the same, so timing cannot matter and the wedge should be exactly zero. The engine returns 0.002 bp instead. That is not an economic effect but an artefact of how year 1 is built: the model gives both of its half-years the same credit intensity, yet prices the first six months off the actual 6M EURIBOR fixing while the second follows from the 1-year swap rate. The two halves are therefore not quite interchangeable, and a flat curve cannot cancel to the last decimal. Remove the fixing and the residue disappears: the wedge is then 0.000 bp.
  • Straight and rising: wedge proportional to the slope.
  • Curved: a curve that flattens at the long end (the usual shape) shrinks the wedge, because the loan's tail lands where costs grow more slowly. A curve that steepens further out enlarges it.
Takeaway

Equal WAL means equal quantity of lending, not equal timing. An amortiser always has its exposure later than the equal-WAL bullet. On any rising curve that alone makes it dearer. The early-year loss and the late-year gain do not cancel, even on a straight line.

05 · Deep dive · The two measures of timing

WAL and the exposure centre, defined properly

Two numbers describe when a facility's money is at risk: the WAL and the exposure centre. Both are averages of dates measured in years, both have appeared already, and they are constantly mistaken for one another. Everything from here on rests on telling them apart, so this section defines each one properly, shows what it looks like, and sets them side by side.

WAL, defined two ways

WAL answers one question: how much lending is there, and for how long? There are two routes to it. They look like different quantities — one watches the loan's balance, the other follows individual euros — and they always land on the same number. Both are set out below, then the reason they agree.

Way 1 · by date — watch the balance

Stand at each date in turn and ask how much is still owed at that moment. Multiply by how long that moment lasts, and add up across the life of the loan. In words: how much is out, and for how long, totted up period by period. Geometrically it is the area under the balance-outstanding curve.

WAL = Σ Oi · a
Oi
The balance outstanding in half-year i, as a fraction of the original principal. It starts at 1 and steps down to 0.
a
The length of each period, 0.5 years. Half-years are used because the loan pays semi-annually.

Units: Oi is a fraction, so fraction × years = years. Note that a division is already hidden inside Oi. Had the balance been written in euros, the sum would come out in euro-years, and you would still have to divide by the principal to get back to years — which is exactly what expressing the balance as a fraction does up front.

Way 2 · by euro — follow the money

Forget the balance and take the euros one at a time. Each euro is drawn on some date and repaid on some later date, and the gap between the two is how long that euro was working. Average that gap over every euro borrowed. In words: the average time a euro stays out.

WAL = average repayment date − average drawdown date = 12.75 − 0.25 = 12.50
average repayment date
Each repayment date weighted by how much principal comes back on it. Here the 46 instalments are equal and evenly spaced, so their weighted average is simply the midpoint of the first and the last: (1.5 + 24) ÷ 2 = 12.75 years.
average drawdown date
The same calculation on the way in. Here half the money is drawn at signing and half at month 6: (½ × 0) + (½ × 0.5) = 0.25 years. Subtracting it is what stops the loan being credited for time before the money was actually advanced.

Units: years − years = years. Both terms are averages weighted by principal, so each has already been divided by the amount borrowed. When everything is drawn at once on day one the second term is zero and WAL is simply the average repayment date — which is why a bullet's WAL equals its maturity.

Why the two agree

Put side by side, the two ways share one thing: both are totalling euro-years — one euro being outstanding for one year. Way 1 collects euro-years by date, Way 2 collects the same euro-years by euro. Neither can be larger than the other, because they are inventories of the same thing.

That also settles the units. The raw total is genuinely in euro-years: this loan consumes 5bn × 12.5 = 62.5bn euro-years. WAL reports that total per euro borrowed, so the euros cancel and what is left is years — read it as "the average euro stayed out for 12.5 years". Both formulas above have that division already built in, which is why neither shows it explicitly.

To see it, picture the loan as a physical stack of money. Stack the principal up with the slice that will be repaid first on top. As each instalment is paid the stack is shaved off the top, so the outline of the stack over time is exactly the balance-outstanding curve. Shade the whole stack over its life: every square of that shape is one euro-year, and the total area is every euro-year the loan consumes. Sweeping that area vertically is Way 1; sweeping it horizontally is Way 2.

That is the whole reason "average time a euro stays out" and "total principal-years per euro" are one number. The picture also shows, at a glance, why WAL is blind to dispersion: an area can be pushed around in time, stretched late or bunched early, without its total changing at all. Two loans with very different shapes can cover identical area — which is exactly the bullet-versus-amortiser case in section 04.

The exposure centre: where the shape balances

The WAL is the area of that stack of money. The exposure centre asks a different question of the same shape: not how big it is, but where it sits. Cut the shape out of card and rest it on a knife edge. The point where it balances is the exposure centre.

This is worth doing for the two shapes section 04 drew. Both carry the same WAL of 12.5 years — the same area, the same euro-years — so by the WAL they are the same loan. They do not balance in the same place.

The definition · as a sum

Go through the half-years one at a time, note the date, and weight it by how much is still outstanding then. Periods carrying a lot of balance pull the average towards themselves; periods carrying none have no say at all.

The difference from WAL is smaller than it looks. Way 1 added up the euro-years in each half-year, Σ Oi·a, and stopped there — a total, with no mention of when any of it happened. The exposure centre takes those same euro-years and tags each one with its date before averaging. That extra factor, the date, is the whole difference between the two measures.

exposure centre = Σ ti·Oi·a ÷ WAL
ti
The middle of half-year i, in years from signing. The middle rather than the end, because the balance sits there for the whole period, not just at its close.
Oi·a
The euro-years consumed in that half-year: the balance outstanding, as a fraction of principal, times the 0.5-year length of the period. Exactly one column of the stack from Way 1.
WAL
The total euro-years, since that is all the WAL is. Dividing by it is what turns a total into an average.

So the whole thing reads: every euro-year, tagged with the date it happened, divided by all the euro-years. Units: (years × years) ÷ years = years. The euro-years cancel, and with them every trace of how much exposure there is. What survives is purely when it sits — which is why this, and not the WAL, is the number the credit curve prices.

The same number · as a split

That sum can be rearranged into two pieces that each mean something on their own: a baseline every loan starts from, and a penalty for spreading repayments out in time. It is the clearest statement of what the exposure centre actually measures, and it is what section 04's rule of thumb rests on.

exposure centre = WAL / 2 + (spread of repayment dates)² ÷ (2 × WAL)
WAL / 2
The baseline: where a loan balances if all its exposure is concentrated on a single date. No loan can sit earlier than this.
spread²
The variance of the repayment dates: spread is their standard deviation, how far they typically sit from the WAL, so squaring it gives years squared. Zero for a bullet, which has one date; large for 46 instalments running over 23 years.

Units: years + (years² ÷ years) = years. That is what the division by 2 × WAL is for — without it the two terms could not be added. Because a variance can never be negative, the second term only ever pushes the centre later.

Where the split comes from

The split is not a second formula. It is the sum above, rewritten in four steps — and the steps are worth following, because they are also where the lone 2 enters.

  1. Take one euro. A euro drawn at the start and repaid after t years is outstanding on every day in between — one horizontal row of the stack in the first diagram. It therefore contributes t euro-years, and since it is out evenly across that whole span, those euro-years are centred on the middle of the row, at t/2. That halving is the only 2 in the formula.
  2. Weigh the euros against each other. Each euro has its own little balance point at t/2, and the balance point of the whole shape is the average of them. Not a plain average, though: a euro that stayed out for 20 years puts 20 euro-years of weight on the shape, while one that stayed out for 2 years puts only 2. So each euro's centre is weighted by its own euro-years — its t — giving Σ t × (t/2) ÷ Σ t.
  3. Swap the sums for averages. The last step uses a fact about averages, so the two sums need to be averages first. That is easy, because a sum is nothing more than the count times the average: with N euros, Σ t = N × average(t) and Σ t² = N × average(t²). The average time a euro stays out is the WAL (Way 2), so the fraction becomes (½ × N × average(t²)) ÷ (N × WAL) — and the N cancels, top against bottom, leaving average(t²) ÷ (2 × WAL).
  4. Split the average of t². One of the standard identities of statistics says the average of the squares equals the variance plus the square of the average: average(t²) = spread² + WAL². Substituting gives (spread² + WAL²) ÷ (2 × WAL), and separating the two terms leaves WAL² ÷ (2 × WAL), which is just WAL/2, plus spread² ÷ (2 × WAL).

So the 2 is never applied twice. There is one division by 2 × WAL, written first as a single fraction and then shared out between the two terms. Steps 1 and 2 together also explain why dispersion can only push the centre later: a euro that stays out twice as long sits twice as late and carries twice the weight, so its pull on the average grows with the square of its life. Stretching a schedule out therefore moves the centre out more than shortening it moves the centre in.

Closed forms for the two basic shapes

For most schedules the sum above is the only way to get the answer. But for the two shapes in the diagram the answer is known in advance, straight from geometry — a formula that hands you the result rather than making you add up sixty terms. That is what a closed form is.

bullet = WAL / 2      straight amortiser = 2 × WAL / 3
bullet
A rectangle balances in the middle. The bullet's exposure is spread evenly from day one to maturity, so it balances at half its length. It is also the split above with a variance of zero: a bullet has a single repayment date, so there is no dispersion to add.
straight amortiser
A triangle balances one third of the way along from its tall side. To hold the same area, the amortiser's triangle has to run twice as long as the bullet, and one third of that double length lands at 2 × WAL / 3.

With WAL = 12.5 years: the bullet balances at 6.25, the amortiser at 8.33. Both figures are marked on the diagram above.

So the three boxes above are not three different quantities. The sum is the definition, the split is that same sum rearranged to show what drives it, and the closed forms are available only when the shape is simple enough to recognise. A real schedule — a drawdown ramp, uneven instalments, a grace period — is not a clean triangle, so the model ignores them and computes the weighted average directly. That is why this loan lands at 8.26 years rather than exactly on the 8.33 the triangle predicts.

06 · Credit, default and recovery

Two questions: how likely is default, and how much is lost

Ideas 1 and 2 were about when a loan is exposed. This section is about what that exposure costs. A lender's margin has to cover its expected loss, and an expected loss is always the same two things multiplied together: the chance the borrower defaults, and the share of the money that does not come back. Everything below is one of those two.

credit margin ≈ λ × (1 − R)   ⇔   λ ≈ credit margin ÷ (1 − R)
λ
How likely. The default intensity, or hazard rate: the chance of defaulting in a given year.
1 − R
How much is lost. The share not recovered: 60% with 40% recovery.
margin
Floating: the Z-spread. Fixed: the YTM minus the swap rate of the same maturity (both in semi-annual terms).

Year 10: the year's own cost is 99.0 bp, so λ ≈ 99 ÷ 0.6 ≈ 165bp; the exact model gives 159.6 bp. Dividing by 0.6 slightly overshoots: what is lost on default is the principal plus the half-year's interest, less recovery, so the exact break-even is c ≈ λ × (1 − R + f·a) ≈ λ × 0.620 here (section 10).

How likely: the default intensity λ

The model expresses each year's credit cost as a default intensity λ, also called a hazard rate.

λyear b = probability of defaulting during year b, given no default so far

λ = 1.596% in year 10 means: if the borrower reaches year 10 alive, there is about a 1.6% chance it defaults during that year. The conditional clause is the important part — every λ is quoted on the assumption that the borrower got that far.

Each λ covers a single year in isolation. Pricing needs two further things: the chance of reaching a date at all — the survival probability S(t) = exp(−Σλ), which is what weights every later cash flow — and a way to recover the λs from the bond quotes in the first place. Both are set out below.

From λ to survival

Each λ describes one year in isolation. To price anything the model needs the opposite: the chance of reaching a date at all, because a margin due in year 20 is only collected if the borrower is still there in year 20. That number is the survival probability S(t), and it follows from the λs by chaining.

The chaining works precisely because each λ is conditional. Surviving to year 3 means surviving year 1, then surviving year 2 given you survived year 1, then year 3 given the first two. Conditional probabilities multiply, so survival is a running product of one-year survival terms — and a product of exponentials is the exponential of a sum, which is what collapses the chain into a single formula.

S(t) = exp(−λ1) × exp(−λ2) × … = exp(−Σ λ up to t)
S(t)
The chance of still being alive at t. Starts at 1 and falls as the λs accumulate. Here S(24) = 0.660, so the market implies a 34% chance of default over the loan's life.
exp(−λ)
The chance of getting through one year at intensity λ: 98.417% in year 10. Why the exponential, rather than simply 1 − λ, is worked through below.
Σ λ
The sum of every yearly intensity up to t. The engine steps in half-years, multiplying by exp(−λ·0.5) each time.

Nothing stops a negative λ feeding through: it makes a term greater than 1 and lifts S above 1, which is why this issuer shows S(1) = 1.0031 (see the warning below).

Why exp, and not simply 1 − λ

Start with the simplest possible picture. If the borrower could only fail on one date — say the last day of the year — then λ would be that single date's probability, and surviving the year would just be 1 − λ: 98.404% in year 10.

But a borrower can fail on any day. So split the year in two and let each half carry half the intensity. Surviving the year now means surviving both halves, (1 − λ/2) × (1 − λ/2), which comes to 98.410% — close to the first answer, but not the same. Split it into months instead and the answer shifts again. Each extra split spreads the same total intensity over more, smaller chances to fail:

The answers climb and then stop moving. That settling value is exp(−λ) — it is simply where (1 − λ/n)n ends up once the slices are small enough that splitting again changes nothing. Taking the exponential is just the compact way of saying "default is allowed at any instant, not only on scheduled dates".

The practical gain is consistency. Because the model steps in half-years, it needs a survival figure that does not depend on how the time is chopped up, and only the exponential has that property: exp(−λ/2) applied twice gives back exp(−λ) exactly, whereas (1 − λ/2)² misses 1 − λ by about 0.6bp. Over 48 half-years those small inconsistencies would accumulate.

How each λ is actually found

The credit-margin relation in the first box is a rule of thumb, and the model does not price off it. It recovers the λs from the bond prices themselves, one year at a time.

The condition it imposes is simple: every quoted bond, valued with the model's own λs, must come back to the price the market actually shows. The quotes are par levels — each Z-spread is the spread that would make a bond of that maturity trade at par — so the target price is 100.

Valuing a bond once default is allowed takes three ingredients, and survival drives two of them:

  • Coupons, which arrive only if the borrower is still alive, so each one is scaled by the survival probability on its date.
  • Recovery, 40% of face, which arrives only if the borrower fails — and the chance of failing in a given half-year is the drop in survival across it.
  • Discounting on the swap curve, applied to both.

That is what makes the calculation unwind so neatly, one year at a time:

  1. The 1-year bond spans only year 1, so its price depends on λ1 and nothing else. One equation, one unknown.
  2. The 2-year bond spans years 1 and 2 — but λ1 is now known, so λ2 is again the only unknown.
  3. Carry on out the curve. Each new bond reaches one year further than the last, so each step adds exactly one new unknown and never more.

This is the same peeling idea as section 03, where each bond's quote was stripped back to the cost of its own final year. What differs is how much each year counts towards the quote it is being stripped out of. Section 03 let every year count the same, which keeps the arithmetic simple enough to follow by hand. Here a year's contribution is discounted, and scaled by the chance the borrower is still alive to reach it, so distant years count for less — which is exactly why the peeled figures there came out below the ones the model actually uses.

First, a word on why λ can be treated as a single number for a whole year. The market supplies one quote per maturity — one figure per year — so there is only enough information to pin down one intensity per year. The model therefore takes λ to be flat within each year and to step at year boundaries. That is an assumption imposed by the data, not something the exponential requires. Count it through: after interpolation the curve supplies one value for each year, so holding λ flat per year leaves exactly one unknown per equation, and the chain has a single answer. Allow λ to move within a year — say a separate value for each half — and that year now carries two unknowns while still offering only one curve value. Twice as many unknowns as equations, and infinitely many λ paths would reprice every bond equally well. Some assumption has to break the tie, and flat-within-the-year is the mildest one available.

With λ flat across the year, both halves of that year carry the same survival factor. Call it u: of the borrowers alive at the start of a half-year, the fraction still alive at its end. Note that u is not S(t), despite both being survival figures. S(t) is a cumulative level — the chance of lasting all the way from signing to t — while u is a single step. S(t) is just every u up to t multiplied together; within year b, S(b) = S(b−1) × u × u.

That is what makes the algebra fall out so neatly. The bond pays twice in the year: once at the half-year, reached with probability u, and once at the year-end, reached with probability u × u. So the unknown never appears in any form more complicated than u and u², which makes the pricing equation an ordinary quadratic — the kind solved with the formula taught at school. Once u is known, λ follows immediately, since u is just exp(−λ/2) seen from the other side.

That is what a closed-form solution means: the answer is produced by evaluating a formula once, rather than being hunted for. The usual alternative in a spreadsheet is Goal Seek, Excel's built-in search: it guesses a λ, reprices the bond, sees how far the price lands from 100, adjusts the guess, and repeats until the gap is small enough to accept. That is iteration — a loop of guess and correct.

Avoiding it is worth more than speed. An iterative search stops at "close enough" rather than exact, can land on slightly different answers depending on where it started and how tight the tolerance was set, and can simply fail to converge on awkward inputs. Solving the quadratic removes all three risks at once: every λ is computed directly, the bonds reprice to their quotes to within about 10−12 bp, and the workbook returns the identical answer every time it is opened — which is what makes the output safe to audit.

Why the workbook shows "Negative forward hazard: YES"

This issuer's 1–3 year bonds trade below swaps: Z-spreads of −19, −15 and −14bp, and YTMs below the swap rates (3.032% against 3.270% at 1 year). The only default intensity that prices a bond richer than the risk-free curve is a negative one, so λ comes out around −31, −18 and −19bp for years 1–3. A real probability cannot be negative, hence the flag.

The calculation stays valid: every bond reprices to its quote and the integrity checks read OK. It simply means the market treats this issuer as safer than the swap curve at the short end, which is common for very high-grade names whose short bonds are scarce or in demand. These are market-implied, not real-world, probabilities

λ is backed out of prices. Bond spreads also pay for uncertainty, liquidity and supply and demand, so implied default probabilities are usually well above historical default rates for the same rating. For this calculator that is exactly right: the goal is to price the loan consistently with where the borrower's bonds trade, whatever is driving those prices. λ is a pricing tool, not a default forecast.

Why the recovery assumption itself barely matters

Changing the recovery rate moves the answer very little: 8.99 bp at 20% recovery, 8.82 at 40%, 8.49 at 60% (section 08). That looks surprising, because R sits inside two formulas on this page — the λ ≈ margin ÷ (1 − R) rule of thumb above, and the per-default loss, 1 + f·a − R, just above that. Change R and both formulas move. Going in, it changes the λ you calibrate; coming out, it changes the loss that λ is used to price.

Going in: R decides how large λ must be

The quoted spread is fixed market data — it does not move just because you change your assumption about R. But λ ≈ margin ÷ (1 − R) does: raise R and (1 − R) shrinks, so matching that same fixed spread now calls for a larger λ. Credit a default with recovering more, in other words, and the model needs defaults to happen more often to still explain the spread actually observed in the market.

Coming out: R decides how large that default's loss is

That larger λ is now used to price the loan, by way of the loss formula above: λ sets Si−1 − Si, so a bigger λ means a bigger chance of default. R, meanwhile, is not finished — it appears again on its own, inside the per-default loss, 1 + f·a − R, still shrinking what each default costs, exactly as it always did. One assumption about R, acting on the same formula twice: once at a distance, by way of λ, and once directly.

Those two effects sit on different factors of one product, probability of default times per-default loss, so pushing one up and the other down mostly offsets rather than compounding. The offset is not exact — the fair price still drifts from 8.99 bp down to 8.49 bp across the 20%–60% range — but it is close enough that the recovery rate, within reason, barely changes the answer.

Takeaway

The recovery rate — the 40% figure — is a soft assumption: it enters the calculation twice, going in and coming out, in ways that offset, so the model absorbs most of whatever rate you choose. The recovery base — original face or outstanding balance, from the subsection above — is not soft at all; it enters only once, with no offsetting error to cancel it. A wrong base does not crash the model or produce an obviously broken number: it quietly makes the late years price as nearly free, by turning their loss given default negative. Sections 03–05 found the wedge precisely in those late years — so a base error would not just bias this section's output, it would erase the thing the whole page is about.

The swap curve: what it is, and the three jobs it does

A par swap rate is the fixed rate at which a plain interest-rate swap, fixed payments against floating 6M EURIBOR, is worth nothing on the day it is struck. The swap curve is that rate for every maturity from 1 to 30 years: here 3.27% at 1 year, rising to 3.51% at 15 and easing to 3.29% at 30 (plotted in section 02). It prices time and nothing else. No borrower is involved, so it carries no credit risk, which is why it is the standard stand-in for the risk-free rate.

The model cannot use par rates as they come. It converts them into two kinds of number, and every later use of the curve takes one or the other:

Bootstrapping λ in closed form

Purpose. Find the one number — λb, the default intensity for year b — that makes the quoted bond maturing at b worth exactly its market price. Years 1…b−1 are already solved, so λb is the only unknown. Within year b it is held constant (section 06), so both half-years share one survival factor: u = e−λb/2, the fraction surviving six months.

The equation. Value the bond, set it equal to its price, and solve. Because the quotes are par levels, that price is 1 per unit of face — which is the left-hand side:

1 = K + DF(b−½)·Sp·[u·g1 + R(1−u)] + DF(b)·Sp·[u²·g2 + R(u − u²)]
1
The bond's price. It is 1 because each quote is the level at which that maturity would trade at par; repricing to par is the condition being imposed.
K
Everything the bond pays before year b, already valued because those λs are known: Σ DF[S·coupon + R·ΔS], where ΔS is the fall in survival across a period — the share of scenarios in which default happens and recovery is paid instead. Floating coupon f·a + q·a, fixed coupon q·a (q = Z-spread or semi-annual YTM).
first bracket
The payment at b−½, discounted by DF(b−½). Two outcomes: survive that half-year, with probability u, and collect coupon g1; or default in it, with probability 1−u, and collect recovery R.
second bracket
The payment at b, discounted by DF(b). Survive both halves, probability u², and collect g2 — the final coupon plus the principal; or get through the first half and fail in the second, probability u − u², and collect R.
Sp
Survival to the start of year b. Both brackets sit behind it because none of it happens unless the borrower reaches year b at all.
DF(b−½), DF(b)
The ordinary discount factors from the block above, read at this year's two payment dates — the same quantity written DFi in the weights later on. For year 1 that means DF(0.5) = 0.984926 and DF(1) = 0.968335.

So the whole right-hand side reads: what the bond has already paid, plus what it pays in its final year under each of the three ways that year can go — survive both halves, fail in the first, fail in the second.

Solving it. u appears only as u and u², so multiplying out the brackets and collecting terms leaves an ordinary quadratic, a2u² + a1u + a0 = 0:

u = (−a1 + √(a1² − 4a2a0)) ÷ 2a2,   λb = −2 ln u
a2
DF(b)Sp(g2−R) — the u² terms: the final payment, net of the recovery that would have been received instead.
a1
Sp(DF(b−½)(g1−R) + DF(b)R) — the u terms, one from each bracket.
a0
K + DF(b−½)SpR − 1 — everything with no u in it, including the price moved across from the left.
which root
The + root. a0 is negative whenever the bond's earlier cash flows are worth less than par, so the two roots straddle zero and only the positive one can be a survival ratio.
λb = −2 ln u
The definition of u read backwards. u = e−λb/2 is the half-year survival factor of section 06, so taking logs gives ln u = −λb/2, and multiplying by −2 isolates λb. The 2 appears because λ is quoted per year while u spans half a year. Solving in u rather than in λ directly is what keeps this closed form: survival enters the bond's price only as u and u², which is a quadratic, whereas the same equation written in λ would carry e−λ/2 and e−λ and need iterating. The log at the end converts back. For year 10, u = 0.992050 gives λ = 159.6 bp, and u² = 0.984164 is that year's full-year survival, exp(−λ).
check
Every quoted bond reprices to its input; the workbook shows the maximum error (about 10−12 bp). Beyond the last quote, the last λ is extended, not the last spread.

Loan profile

Purpose. Turn the drawdown and repayment schedule into one number per half-year: Oi, the balance outstanding in period i as a fraction of the full amount. This is the only place the shape of the loan enters the calculation. Write x for the length of the drawdown period in years and y for the length of the repayment period, so this case has x = 1 and y = 23.

Oi = min(i, D)/D − max(0, min(i−1, n) − D)/(2y)
D = 2x drawdowns,   n = 2x + 2y,   WAL = Σ Oi·a
drawn
The fraction advanced by period i. Drawdowns of 1/D arrive at t = 0, 0.5, …, x − 0.5 (a single drawdown of 1 at t = 0 if x = 0). The min caps it at 1: once the facility is fully drawn it stays drawn.
repaid
The fraction paid back by then, in instalments of 1/(2y) at t = x + 0.5, …, x + y. The max floors it at 0, which stops repayment being counted before the drawdown period ends, and the inner min stops it running past final maturity.
D, n
D is the number of half-years in the drawdown period, n the number to final maturity. Here D = 2 and n = 48.
WAL
Σ Oi·a — the area under this balance profile, which is the "by date" definition from section 05.

Per-period break-even cost (exact decomposition)

Purpose. Convert each half-year's default intensity into the margin that exactly covers its expected loss. This is the per-year cost that section 03 peels out of the curve, now stated exactly.

Why call it a cost? It is one number read from either side of the table. To the lender it is break-even compensation — the least that makes lending over that period worth doing. To the borrower it is what that stretch of funding costs. This page uses the borrower's word throughout, because the question it asks is what the loan costs measured against the borrower's own bond curve. And because it is a break-even, it contains no profit margin, no capital charge and no funding spread beyond credit, which is precisely what qualifies it as the yardstick for a fair price: it is what the borrower's own bonds say the risk is worth, and nothing else.

ci = (1 − pi)·(1 + fia − R) ÷ (pi·a),   pi = Si/Si−1
1 − p
Chance of defaulting in the half-year, given survival to its start. Note pi is the same quantity as u in the bootstrap above — the half-year survival ratio — since within year b, Si/Si−1 = e−λb/2 = u.
1 + fa − R
What is lost if default happens: principal plus the forward interest due, less recovery. The numerator as a whole is therefore the expected loss over the period: chance of default times loss given default.
÷ pi·a
Two conversions, and the reason the formula is not simply the expected loss. Dividing by a restates a half-year amount as a rate per annum. Dividing by p grosses the margin up: it is charged as a running spread and is therefore only collected if the borrower survives the period, so it has to cover the whole expected loss out of the surviving fraction alone. Setting margin collected equal to loss expected, c·a·p = (1 − p)(1 + fa − R), and solving for c gives exactly this.
sanity check
Because p ≈ 1 − λa, this is close to c ≈ λ(1 − R + fa), slightly above the textbook λ(1 − R).
ki
The period's total cost on that same reading, and the quantity the fair price averages. Floating: ki = ci — credit alone, because a floating loan passes the base rate straight through to the borrower, so the only thing the lender prices is the margin over it. Fixed: ki = fi + ci — the risk-free forward for that period plus its credit cost, because a fixed loan quotes one all-in rate and therefore has to price the interest rate too. For the half-year ending at year 20: floating k = 145.8bp, fixed k = 3.2268% + 1.5672% = 4.7940%.

Fair price and saving

Purpose. Three steps, in order: decide how much each half-year counts, average the per-period costs using those weights to get the loan's fair price, then turn the gap against the price actually charged into money. N is the amount of the facility, EUR 5bn here.

wi = DFi·Si·a·Oi    fair = Σ wiki ÷ Σ wi    PV saving = N · Σ wi(ki − charged)
wi
The weight on half-year i: how much is outstanding (Oi), for how long (a), worth what today (DFi), and only if the borrower is still there (Si). Σ wi is the risky annuity, 9.002 for this loan.
ki
The cost of half-year i, carried over from the block above: the credit margin alone on a floating loan, or the risk-free forward plus credit on a fixed one. That difference is why the floating answer comes out as a spread (62.32 bp) and the fixed one as an all-in rate (4.1355%) — the two are not quoting the same thing.
fair
The weighted average of those per-period costs — one flat spread or rate equivalent to paying each period its own cost. Equivalent to solving PV(loan) = PV(drawdowns) for that flat number, where PV(loan) = Σ DFi[Si(couponiOi + Pi) + R(Si−1 − Si)Oi], Pi being the principal repaid in period i, and drawdowns are valued at DF·S on their dates. The two routes agree because the per-period par conditions telescope; the workbook checks it to about 10−7 euros.
PV saving
Each half-year contributes the gap between what it costs and what is charged, weighted and discounted; N scales the per-unit answer to the size of the facility. Dividing the same sum by Σ wi instead gives the saving in basis points a year.
split
Summing over periods before and after the WAL date (the straddling period split pro rata in time) gives the loss and benefit phases, which add up exactly to the net.
charged
The shortcut price: linear interpolation of the quoted curve at the exact WAL. For a fixed loan the quoted annual yield is first restated on the loan's semi-annual basis as 2((1 + Y/m)m/2 − 1), where Y is that annual yield and m = 1 for EUR bonds.

11 · Questions people ask

Questions and answers

What if the bond curve is flat? What if it is straight?

Flat: every year costs the same, so it does not matter when the money is lent and the shortcut is right (saving ≈ 0; 0.002 bp in this case, a modelling residue). Straight and rising: there is a saving roughly equal to slope × WAL ÷ 3, because the amortiser's exposure sits later than the bullet's. Curvature then adds (steepening) or subtracts (flattening). See section 04.

Isn't the WAL already a centre of gravity? Why is it different?

WAL is the centre of gravity of the repayments. The credit curve prices the balance outstanding, whose centre of gravity is WAL/2 for a bullet and about 2·WAL/3 for a straight amortiser (8.26 years for this loan vs 6.25 for the bullet). There is no standard name; "exposure-weighted average time" describes it. Macaulay duration is the textbook relative.

Does the model recompute a centre of gravity for every half-year?

No. The centre of gravity is a teaching device and is exact only for straight curves. The model never computes it. It takes each half-year's balance (from the schedule, computed once), each half-year's cost (from the curve) and each half-year's weight, and averages. That works for any curve shape.

Where do the "share of weighting" percentages come from?

Each half-year's weight is balance × 0.5 × discount factor × survival. A block's share is its weights divided by the total, 9.002. The 18–24 year block holds 6.8% of the principal-years but only 3.5% of the weight, because those euros are far in the future and only collected if the borrower survives. The share is not multiplied by the balance again, and the block's cost (153.5 bp) comes from the curve, not from the loan.

Which "spread" drives λ: the Z-spread or the whole YTM?

Floating: the Z-spread directly, since it is already a spread over swaps. Fixed: the YTM minus the swap rate of the same maturity. The model does not subtract explicitly; it values fixed bonds on the swap curve and attributes whatever yield the swap curve cannot explain to λ. At 1 year: YTM 3.032% annual = 3.009% semi-annual, minus swap 3.270%, gives about −26bp of credit.

Is λ a real probability of default?

No, it is market-implied. It includes everything investors charge for, not only expected losses, so it is usually well above historical default rates, and here it is even negative at the short end. It is the right input for pricing consistently with the borrower's bonds, and the wrong input for a credit risk forecast.

Why does the workbook warn "Negative forward hazard"?

Because the short bonds trade below swaps. A bond can only be richer than the risk-free curve if the implied default intensity is negative. The pricing remains internally consistent; the flag asks you to confirm the data (same swap curve as the Z-spreads, same date).

Why is row 12 different from row 11 when the margin X is zero?

Floating: it isn't; 53.5 bp and 0.5350% are the same number. Fixed: the annual-coupon bond yield (4.0965%) is restated as the half-yearly rate with the same effective yield (4.0554%), so it can be compared with the fair rate, which is computed on the loan's half-yearly payments.

What is the risky annuity in row 9?

The sum of all half-year weights: the loan's principal-years after discounting and default risk. It converts basis points into today's money: 1bp a year on this loan is worth 1bp × 9.002 × EUR 5bn ≈ €4.5m. In CDS markets the same quantity is called the risky PV01 (RPV01).

What is the 6M fixing for?

The swap curve starts at 1 year, but the model needs a discount factor every six months. The 6M EURIBOR fixing gives the actual rate for the first six months instead of interpolating between today and the 1-year point. For a floating loan it barely matters (0.0004bp here). For a fixed loan it does: it pins the first half-year's forward, which is part of the fixed rate, and leaving it out moves the fixed saving by about 0.5bp (8.02 to 7.51). Enter the real fixing, from the same date as the rest of the curve.

How much does the swap curve matter? Can we use free data?

Very little for the result: a 30bp error in the whole curve, 6M fixing included, moves the floating saving from 8.82 to 8.70bp and the fixed from 8.02 to 8.06bp. Section 06 shows why: the pieces move, the total hardly does. Free sources with a short delay are adequate, provided the 6M fixing and the Z-spreads are from the same date as the curve. The bond curves are what drive the answer.

Why are the loss and the benefit not equal on a straight curve?

Because the loan's exposure centre is later than the equal-WAL bullet's. The benefit side reaches further up the curve than the loss side reaches down it. See the rectangle-and-triangle figure in section 04.

Why is the fixed "risk-free effect" negative here?

The swap curve rises to 3.51% at 15 years and then falls to 3.29% at 30. Risk-free forwards in the loan's late years are therefore below the 12.5-year par rate. Reading the fixed rate at 12.5 years slightly overstates the risk-free part for this profile (−1.23 bp), while the credit part is understated by 9.25 bp. Net, the borrower still saves 8.02 bp.

Does the lender lose money?

Not in an accounting sense. The saving is margin the lender forgoes relative to pricing the loan consistently with the borrower's bond curve. Whether that is acceptable depends on relationship, ancillary business, funding and capital, none of which this calculator models. Use it to know the price of the convention, then decide.

What does recovery change?

Almost nothing for the result: from 8.99 bp (20%) to 8.49 bp (60%) on the floating side. A higher recovery raises the implied λ and lowers the loss per default by the same proportion. What matters is applying recovery to the balance outstanding, not to the original amount.

Assumptions and limitations

  • Bond quotes as par bonds. Each Z-spread is treated as the spread of a floating bullet at par for that tenor, each YTM as the coupon of a par bullet. Exact for bonds near par; for deep discount or premium lines, prefer a fitted issuer curve (Bloomberg CRVF / BVAL).
  • Loan mechanics. Semi-annual periods, accrual 0.5; floating resets to the 6M forward. Interest on drawn balances only; no commitment fee unless entered as a fee. Drawdowns are equal half-yearly amounts from signing.
  • Fixed rate basis. The charged fixed rate is the semi-annual equivalent of YTM(WAL) + X. If the term sheet uses the annual number as a half-yearly coupon, add the difference through X.
  • Single curve. The same swap curve projects and discounts. No basis between loan and bond funding, no bond new-issue premium unless entered (Inputs rows 22–24).
  • One intensity per year. λ is held flat within each year and steps at year boundaries, because the curve supplies one quote per maturity and so identifies one intensity per year. A genuinely flat curve therefore still leaves a residue of about 0.002bp rather than exactly zero.
  • Beyond the data. Inside the quotes, years are interpolated linearly; beyond the last bond quote the last λ is extended and flagged. Maximum life 30 years.
  • Validation. The engine reproduces the reference specification exactly (65.8781bp vs 55.0000bp, wedge +10.8781bp) and, for this case, matches the workbook to the cent. Integrity checks in the workbook: bond repricing error, PV identity, WAL cross-check.
